# Context
Acme Corp's Enterprise Integrations team, led by their manager Picard, specializes in building and maintaining integrations between Carboni and large enterprise customers. These integrations are complex, high-stakes projects where mistakes can jeopardize multi-million dollar contracts. Picard built this team from the ground up and personally led the first five major enterprise integrations, establishing a reputation for technical excellence and attention to detail.

The team has grown to nine engineers, including several strong senior engineers who have successfully delivered multiple projects. Geordi, one of the senior engineers, recently completed a complex integration with a major financial institution three weeks ahead of schedule. Crusher, another senior engineer, has become the go-to expert for security compliance requirements. The team is clearly capable of operating with more autonomy.

However, Picard remains deeply involved in the details of every project. He reviews all technical designs, participates in most customer calls, and personally approves all major decisions. Team members must often wait hours or days for Picard to review their work before proceeding. Some engineers have started making decisions without his input to maintain momentum, then informing him after the fact. This creates tension when Picard discovers details he would have handled differently.

Recently, the situation came to a head. Geordi's team made an architectural decision while Picard was out sick for three days. When Picard returned and learned about it, he disagreed with the approach and asked them to redesign it. Geordi was frustrated, arguing the decision was sound and the redesign would delay the project by two weeks. "You hired us to be senior engineers," Geordi said. "But you won't let us engineer."

Beverly, the VP of Engineering, had a candid conversation with Picard after this incident. She acknowledged his technical excellence and the high quality his team produces, but noted that his involvement in every decision has become a bottleneck. Two promising enterprise deals are on hold waiting for Picard's team to have capacity. She needs the Enterprise Integrations team to scale their impact, which means Picard needs to delegate more effectively.

# Challenge
Picard knows Beverly is right, but he's genuinely concerned about quality and customer relationships. The enterprise customers expect Acme's best, and he's seen how costly mistakes can be in this domain. How can he maintain the team's high standards while giving engineers more autonomy? Where should he stay involved, and where should he step back? And how can he rebuild trust with Geordi and others who feel constrained?

# Discussion Prompts
- What are the risks of Picard's current approach? What are the risks of delegating more?
- How might Picard's past success as a technical leader be influencing his current behavior? Is this helping or hindering the team?
- What might "good" delegation look like for Picard? What would he need to do differently? What would his team members need to do differently?
- How can Picard maintain quality standards while increasing autonomy? What mechanisms or processes might help?
- What should Picard do to repair the relationship with Geordi specifically? What about team trust more broadly?
- How might Picard's role need to evolve as the team grows? What skills does he need to develop?
- What support or feedback does Picard need from Beverly or other leaders to make this transition successfully?
- Are there situations where Picard should remain deeply involved? How can he identify which decisions require his involvement and which don't?
