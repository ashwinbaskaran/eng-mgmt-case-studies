# Kathryn Case Study: Coach Guide

## Key Management Learnings

This section outlines the critical management concepts and skills that students should develop through discussion of this case study.

### 1. **Retention Starts Long Before People Consider Leaving**

**Core Learning:** By the time someone is "considering leaving within a year," the root causes have been developing for months. Great managers prevent retention problems through ongoing relationship-building.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Survey response is a lagging indicator, not the start of the problem
- People don't suddenly decide to leave; dissatisfaction builds gradually
- Proactive management catches issues early when they're easier to address
- Retention is not a program or initiative—it's daily management practice

**Key Insight:** The question isn't just "What should Kathryn do now?" but "What should Kathryn have been doing all along?" Regular 1:1s, career conversations, pulse checks, and psychological safety create conditions where problems surface early and are addressed before people start job searching.

---

### 2. **Psychological Safety Enables Honest Feedback**

**Core Learning:** People need to feel safe to be honest. Without psychological safety, managers only learn about problems when it's too late.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- If team members feel safe, they raise concerns directly instead of suffering silently
- Psychological safety is built through consistent behavior over time
- Managers create safety by responding well to bad news, admitting mistakes, and being vulnerable
- Anonymity requirement suggests trust deficit

**Key Insight:** The fact that these concerns surfaced in an anonymous survey, not in 1:1s, tells us something important. Either Kathryn's 1:1s weren't deep enough, or team members didn't feel safe being honest. Both are fixable, but they require intentional work.

---

### 3. **Anonymous Feedback Reveals Patterns, Not Individuals**

**Core Learning:** Anonymous surveys are designed to protect individuals while revealing organizational patterns. Trying to "figure out who" defeats the purpose.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Anonymity is a feature, not a bug
- The goal is understanding themes, not identifying people
- Approaching the problem as detective work will backfire
- Trust is broken when managers try to de-anonymize

**Key Insight:** Kathryn should treat the survey as saying "Your team has retention risk" not "Specific people are leaving." The response should be team-level improvement, not individual targeting. If someone wants to be identified, they'll speak up when Kathryn creates space for it.

---

### 4. **Engagement and Investigation Require Different Approaches**

**Core Learning:** How you investigate affects whether people will be honest and whether trust increases or decreases.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- 1:1 conversations focused on "who is unhappy" will feel interrogative
- 1:1 conversations focused on "how can I support you better" feel caring
- Team conversations can normalize discussion and reduce isolation
- The method should match the goal: understanding issues, not identifying individuals

**Key Insight:** A better framing: "The survey showed some people are thinking about leaving. That concerns me because I care about this team. I want to understand what we can improve. I'm going to ask each of you: What would make this the best team you've ever been on? What frustrates you? How can I support you better?" This invites conversation without demanding confession.

---

### 5. **Multiple Data Sources Provide Richer Understanding**

**Core Learning:** Don't rely on a single data point. Triangulate using multiple sources to understand what's really happening.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Survey data shows what, not why
- Other survey questions might provide context (look for patterns)
- 1:1 conversations, team discussions, and observation all contribute
- Cross-referencing data reveals deeper truth

**Key Insight:** Kathryn should ask: What else was in the survey? How did the team respond to other questions (engagement, satisfaction, work-life balance)? How does BetaTech compare to other teams? Are there patterns by tenure, role, or team sub-group? This analysis might reveal "junior engineers on BetaTech struggle with growth opportunities" or "team is burned out after the big launch"—much more actionable than "some people might leave."

---

### 6. **Transparency Builds Trust, Secrecy Erodes It**

**Core Learning:** When managers hide survey results, teams assume the worst. Sharing results (thoughtfully) demonstrates trust and invites collaboration on solutions.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Hiding survey results suggests management doesn't trust team or wants to hide problems
- Sharing results says "We're in this together"
- Transparency should be balanced with care (don't create panic)
- Teams can be partners in solving problems, not just sources of problems

**Key Insight:** Kathryn should share the results: "The survey showed a few people on our team are considering leaving within the next year. That concerns me. I want to understand what's driving this and what we can do better. I'm not trying to identify who responded what—I want to understand what we as a team need." This frames it as a team challenge, not a witch hunt.

---

### 7. **Retention Strategies Must Address Root Causes**

**Core Learning:** Generic retention tactics fail. Effective retention addresses the specific reasons people are dissatisfied.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- "Pizza parties" don't fix career development concerns
- Raises don't fix toxic culture
- Different people leave for different reasons
- Solution must match the actual problem

**Key Insight:** Kathryn must diagnose before prescribing. If people are leaving because they don't see growth opportunities, she needs career development plans. If they're burned out, she needs workload management. If they're underpaid, she needs compensation adjustments. If they don't feel valued, she needs recognition and appreciation. Generic retention programs waste time and money.

---

### 8. **Proactive Management Beats Reactive Crisis Response**

**Core Learning:** The best retention plan is excellent day-to-day management. By the time you're "developing a retention plan," you're playing catch-up.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Great managers have ongoing career conversations (quarterly or more)
- Great managers know what motivates each team member
- Great managers create growth opportunities proactively
- Great managers build relationships where honesty is normal

**Key Insight:** The case asks: "What might Kathryn need to have been doing before the survey?" This is the key question. Regular 1:1s that go beyond project status. Understanding each person's career goals and creating paths toward them. Recognizing contributions. Building team culture. Managing workload. If Kathryn had been doing these things, issues would have surfaced early: "Hey Kathryn, I'm feeling stuck in my growth" is much easier to address than "I'm interviewing at other companies."

## Facilitation Overview

### Session Structure (Recommended: 60-75 minutes)

1. **Introduction** (5 min)
   - Frame the case and learning objectives
   - Set discussion norms

2. **Understanding the Challenge** (10 min)
   - Why is anonymity challenging?
   - What might be causing the concern?

3. **Investigation Approaches** (15 min)
   - How can Kathryn learn more?
   - Team vs. individual approaches

4. **Prevention and Proactive Management** (15-20 min)
   - What should Kathryn have been doing?
   - Best practices for retention

5. **Response Strategy** (10 min)
   - What should Kathryn do now?
   - How to turn crisis into learning

6. **Synthesis and Takeaways** (10 min)
   - Key learnings
   - Application to students' contexts

---

## Facilitation Guide

### Opening the Discussion

**Goal:** Surface the tension between anonymity and need for information

**Suggested Opening:**
> "Kathryn faces a challenge many managers encounter: anonymous feedback that something is wrong, but not enough information to know what. How does she learn more without violating the anonymity that enabled the feedback in the first place?"

**Initial Poll:**
- "By show of hands: Have you ever been dissatisfied at work but not felt comfortable telling your manager?"

*This personal connection helps students empathize with the engineers who responded to the survey.*

---

### Discussion Flow

#### Phase 1: Why Is This Challenging? (10 min)

**Prompt:** "The case says it's challenging for Kathryn to respond given that the survey is anonymous. Why is that challenging? What does anonymity prevent her from knowing?"

**Guided Questions:**
- "What does Kathryn need to know that she doesn't know?"
- "Why do companies use anonymous surveys in the first place?"
- "What's the purpose of anonymity?"

**Create Two Columns:**

| **What Anonymity Protects** | **What Anonymity Prevents** |
|-----------------------------|-----------------------------|
| • Individual retaliation | • Knowing who is unhappy |
| • Honest feedback | • Understanding individual situations |
| • Psychological safety | • Targeted interventions |
| • Vulnerable sharing | • Immediate conversation with affected people |

**Critical Question:**
> "Given these tradeoffs, how should Kathryn approach this? What would respect the intent of anonymity while also allowing her to address the problem?"

**Answer to Draw Out:** Focus on understanding themes and patterns, not identifying individuals. Create conditions where people can choose to raise concerns if they want, but don't force it.

---

#### Phase 2: Possible Root Causes (10 min)

**Prompt:** "Before we talk about how Kathryn should investigate, let's brainstorm: What might cause engineers on a successful team to consider leaving?"

**Facilitate Brainstorm (Capture All Ideas):**

**Career and Growth:**
- Lack of advancement opportunities
- Not learning new skills
- Unclear career path
- Want to try new type of work

**Work Environment:**
- Burnout or overwork
- Stress and pressure
- Work-life balance issues
- Pace unsustainable after big push

**Relationships:**
- Conflict with team members
- Don't feel valued or appreciated
- Lack of connection with manager
- Team culture issues

**Organizational:**
- Compensation below market
- Company direction concerns
- Reorganization or uncertainty
- Better opportunities elsewhere

**The Work Itself:**
- No longer interested in domain
- Work has become repetitive
- Want different challenges
- Initial excitement worn off

**Discussion:**
- "Which of these can a manager like Kathryn address? Which are beyond her control?"
- "How might Kathryn figure out which of these is the actual issue?"

**Key Point:** Different root causes require different solutions. Diagnosis before prescription.

---

#### Phase 3: Investigation Approaches (15 min)

**Prompt:** "Let's explore different ways Kathryn could investigate. For each approach, let's consider: What information does it provide? What are the risks?"

**Option 1: Share Results + Team Discussion**

**Approach:** Kathryn shares survey results with team and facilitates discussion

**Pros:**
- Transparency builds trust
- Normalizes the conversation
- Team can collaborate on solutions
- Shows Kathryn cares and is listening

**Cons:**
- Some people won't speak up in group setting
- Could create anxiety for whole team
- Might feel like fishing for who responded
- Requires skillful facilitation

**How To Do Well:**
- Frame as "How can we improve?" not "Who is leaving?"
- Create psychological safety in the conversation
- Use small groups or anonymous input methods
- Follow up with 1:1s

---

**Option 2: Individual 1:1 Conversations**

**Approach:** Kathryn has focused 1:1s with each team member

**Pros:**
- Private setting may feel safer
- Can tailor conversation to individual
- Opportunity to strengthen relationship
- Can address individual concerns directly

**Cons:**
- Could feel interrogative if framed wrong
- May feel like trying to identify survey respondents
- Time-consuming
- Requires people to volunteer information

**How To Do Well:**
- Frame as "How can I support you better?" not "Are you leaving?"
- Ask everyone, not just suspected individuals
- Focus on forward-looking questions (growth, satisfaction, needs)
- Listen more than talk

---

**Option 3: Anonymous Follow-Up Survey**

**Approach:** Create additional survey to understand issues better

**Pros:**
- Maintains anonymity
- Can ask targeted questions
- Quantitative data for patterns
- Shows responsiveness to initial survey

**Cons:**
- Impersonal
- Survey fatigue
- Doesn't build relationships
- Delays action

**How To Do Well:**
- Keep it short and focused
- Ask specific questions about potential causes
- Share results and planned actions
- Follow up with personal conversations

---

**Option 4: Analyze Existing Survey Data**

**Approach:** Look at other questions in survey for patterns

**Pros:**
- No additional burden on team
- May reveal patterns (correlation with other responses)
- Can compare to other teams
- Data already exists

**Cons:**
- Limited to what was asked
- May not provide enough insight
- Doesn't involve the team
- Purely analytical, not relational

**How To Do Well:**
- Look for correlations (engagement, satisfaction, work-life balance)
- Compare BetaTech to company-wide
- Identify any demographic patterns (tenure, role)
- Use insights to inform conversation approach

---

**Best Practice to Surface:**
> "Notice these aren't mutually exclusive. Kathryn could analyze survey data AND have team discussion AND do thoughtful 1:1s. What might a comprehensive approach look like?"

**Answer:** Start with data analysis to understand patterns. Share results with team and invite collective problem-solving. Follow up with enriched 1:1 conversations focused on each person's experience and needs.

---

#### Phase 4: What Kathryn Should Have Been Doing (15-20 min)

**Prompt:** "The case asks: What might Kathryn need to have been doing before the survey to be effective in responding now? This is a critical question. What's the answer?"

**Facilitate Building This List:**

**Regular, Meaningful 1:1s:**
- Scheduled consistently (not cancelled often)
- More than project status—include career, satisfaction, challenges
- Create space for concerns and feedback
- Build relationship where honesty is normal

**Career Development:**
- Understand each person's goals and aspirations
- Create development plans and growth opportunities
- Discuss progression regularly (not just annual review)
- Advocate for team members' advancement

**Recognition and Appreciation:**
- Acknowledge contributions regularly
- Celebrate successes as team
- Ensure people feel valued
- Recognize different working styles and contributions

**Work-Life Balance:**
- Monitor for burnout signals
- Respect boundaries
- Model healthy work habits
- Adjust workload when needed

**Psychological Safety:**
- Respond well to bad news
- Admit own mistakes
- Encourage dissent and debate
- Make it safe to raise concerns

**Team Culture:**
- Build connections among team members
- Address conflicts early
- Create inclusive environment
- Maintain transparency about changes and decisions

**Critical Discussion:**
> "If Kathryn had been doing all of this, would she have been surprised by the survey? Why or why not?"

**Answer to Draw Out:** Probably not. These practices create conditions where people raise concerns early: "I'm feeling burned out" or "I don't see my next step" or "I'm getting recruiter calls that are tempting." Then Kathryn can address issues before people start seriously considering leaving.

**Key Teaching Point:**
> "This is the difference between reactive and proactive management. Reactive: survey reveals problem, scramble to fix it. Proactive: continuous relationship-building means you know about issues early and address them before they become retention risks."

---

#### Phase 5: Turning Crisis into Learning (10 min)

**Prompt:** "The case asks: Are there things that could help turn this potential crisis into a learning and strengthening experience? What might that look like?"

**Guided Questions:**
- "How could Kathryn's response actually strengthen the team?"
- "What would good look like here?"
- "What's the opportunity in this situation?"

**Potential Positive Outcomes:**
- Demonstrates that feedback matters and leadership responds
- Opportunity to improve team practices everyone will benefit from
- Strengthens relationships through honest conversation
- Addresses issues that may have been festering
- Builds muscle for giving and receiving feedback
- Creates more psychological safety if done well

**How to Achieve This:**
- Be transparent about the survey and her concern
- Invite team collaboration on solutions
- Take action on feedback and communicate what's changing
- Check in regularly on whether improvements are working
- Model vulnerability and learning mindset

**Example Framing:**
> "The survey told us we have work to do as a team. I'm grateful for that feedback. Let's figure this out together. What would make this the best team you've ever been on?"

---

### Handling Difficult Moments

#### If Students Suggest Kathryn Should Figure Out Who Responded

**Intervention:**
> "I hear you suggesting Kathryn should identify who is considering leaving. Let's think about that. What happens to trust if people believe their anonymous feedback wasn't really anonymous? What happens to future survey participation?"

*Redirect to respecting anonymity and focusing on themes.*

#### If Students Minimize the Concern ("Only a few people")

**Intervention:**
> "The case says 'a few engineers.' On a team that created a 10x improvement framework, losing even one key person might be significant. What's the real cost of turnover? Not just the individual leaving, but the impact on the team?"

*Discuss: recruitment costs, knowledge loss, team morale, workload on remaining members.*

#### If Discussion Focuses Only on Retention Tactics, Not Root Causes

**Intervention:**
> "I'm hearing ideas like raises, bonuses, perks. These might help, but what if the root cause is something different—like lack of growth opportunities or burnout? What happens then?"

*Guide toward diagnostic thinking before solutions.*

#### If Students Don't Connect to "Before the Survey"

**Intervention:**
> "We've talked a lot about what Kathryn should do now. But the case explicitly asks what she should have been doing before. Why is that question in here? What's the lesson?"

*Emphasize prevention through proactive management.*

## Key Teaching Points to Ensure Coverage

Make sure these concepts emerge during discussion:

### ✓ **Retention is proactive, not reactive**
- Built through daily management practices
- Great 1:1s prevent retention problems
- By the time someone is looking, you're late

### ✓ **Psychological safety enables honest feedback**
- People need to feel safe to raise concerns
- Built through consistent behavior over time
- Without it, problems stay hidden until too late

### ✓ **Respect anonymity, focus on themes**
- Don't try to de-anonymize survey responses
- Look for patterns and system-level issues
- Create conditions where people can choose to share

### ✓ **Diagnosis before prescription**
- Different problems need different solutions
- Investigate root causes, don't assume
- Use multiple data sources

### ✓ **Transparency builds trust**
- Sharing results shows respect for team
- Invites collaboration on solutions
- Secrecy creates anxiety and speculation

### ✓ **Turn crisis into opportunity**
- Can strengthen team if handled well
- Demonstrates that feedback leads to action
- Builds muscle for healthy conflict

> "What's the most important thing you learned about retention from this discussion?"

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

### ❌ **Treating Anonymity as a Problem to Solve**
It's a feature. Respect it.

### ❌ **Jumping to Solutions Without Diagnosis**
Must understand root cause first.

### ❌ **Focusing Only on Response, Not Prevention**
"What should Kathryn have been doing before?" is critical.

### ❌ **Generic Retention Tactics**
Pizza parties don't fix career development concerns.

### ❌ **Assuming All Retention Issues Are the Same**
Different people leave for different reasons.

### ❌ **Underestimating Impact of Losing "Just a Few"**
On a high-performing team, each person matters.

## Assessment: What Good Discussion Looks Like

Students are learning well if they:

- ✅ Understand why anonymity is both helpful and challenging
- ✅ Respect the need to focus on themes, not identify individuals
- ✅ Explore root causes before proposing solutions
- ✅ Emphasize prevention through proactive management
- ✅ Recognize the importance of psychological safety
- ✅ See the opportunity in the crisis
- ✅ Connect to their own experiences with managers
- ✅ Commit to specific practices for their own teams

## Follow-Up Activities (Optional)

### Writing Exercise
Ask students to write:
1. The message Kathryn should send to her team about the survey (250 words)
2. A guide for conducting retention-focused 1:1s (300 words)
3. A 90-day plan for improving team engagement

### Small Group Work
Divide into groups:
- Group A: You are team members. What would you want Kathryn to do?
- Group B: Design the follow-up survey Kathryn could use
- Group C: Create a proactive retention checklist for managers

### Role-Play Exercise
Set up conversations:
- Person 1: Kathryn, Person 2: Team member in a retention-focused 1:1
- Person 1: Kathryn, Person 2: Whole team in discussion about survey
