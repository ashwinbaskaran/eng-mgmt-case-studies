# Kathryn Case Study: Coach Guide

## Key Management Learnings

### 1. Retention Starts Long Before People Consider Leaving
**Core Learning:** By the time someone is "considering leaving within a year," the root causes have been developing for months. Great managers prevent retention problems through ongoing relationship-building.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Survey response is a lagging indicator, not the start of the problem
- People don't suddenly decide to leave; dissatisfaction builds gradually
- Proactive management catches issues early when they're easier to address
- Retention is daily management practice, not a program

**Key Insight:** The question isn't just "What should Kathryn do now?" but "What should Kathryn have been doing all along?" Regular 1:1s, career conversations, and psychological safety create conditions where problems surface early.

### 2. Psychological Safety Enables Honest Feedback
**Core Learning:** People need to feel safe to be honest. Without psychological safety, managers only learn about problems when it's too late.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- If team members feel safe, they raise concerns directly instead of suffering silently
- Psychological safety is built through consistent behavior over time
- Managers create safety by responding well to bad news and being vulnerable
- Anonymity requirement suggests trust deficit

**Key Insight:** The fact that concerns surfaced in an anonymous survey, not in 1:1s, tells us something important. Either Kathryn's 1:1s weren't deep enough, or team members didn't feel safe.

### 3. Anonymous Feedback Reveals Patterns, Not Individuals
**Core Learning:** Anonymous surveys are designed to protect individuals while revealing organizational patterns. Trying to "figure out who" defeats the purpose.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Anonymity is a feature, not a bug
- The goal is understanding themes, not identifying people
- Trust is broken when managers try to de-anonymize
- If someone wants to be identified, they'll speak up when space is created

**Key Insight:** Kathryn should treat the survey as saying "Your team has retention risk" not "Specific people are leaving." The response should be team-level improvement.

### 4. Engagement and Investigation Require Different Approaches
**Core Learning:** How you investigate affects whether people will be honest and whether trust increases or decreases.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- 1:1s focused on "who is unhappy" feel interrogative
- 1:1s focused on "how can I support you better" feel caring
- Team conversations can normalize discussion
- The method should match the goal

**Key Insight:** Better framing: "The survey showed some people are thinking about leaving. I want to understand what we can improve. What would make this the best team you've ever been on?"

### 5. Multiple Data Sources Provide Richer Understanding
**Core Learning:** Don't rely on a single data point. Triangulate using multiple sources to understand what's really happening.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Survey data shows what, not why
- Other survey questions might provide context
- 1:1 conversations, team discussions, and observation all contribute
- Cross-referencing data reveals deeper truth

**Key Insight:** Kathryn should ask: What else was in the survey? How did the team respond to other questions? Are there patterns by tenure, role, or sub-group?

### 6. Transparency Builds Trust, Secrecy Erodes It
**Core Learning:** When managers hide survey results, teams assume the worst. Sharing results thoughtfully demonstrates trust and invites collaboration.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Hiding results suggests management doesn't trust team
- Sharing results says "We're in this together"
- Transparency should be balanced with care
- Teams can be partners in solving problems

**Key Insight:** Kathryn should share: "The survey showed a few people are considering leaving. That concerns me. I want to understand what's driving this and what we can do better. I'm not trying to identify who—I want to understand what we as a team need."

### 7. Retention Strategies Must Address Root Causes
**Core Learning:** Generic retention tactics fail. Effective retention addresses the specific reasons people are dissatisfied.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- "Pizza parties" don't fix career development concerns
- Raises don't fix toxic culture
- Different people leave for different reasons
- Solution must match the actual problem

**Key Insight:** Kathryn must diagnose before prescribing. If people lack growth opportunities, she needs career development plans. If they're burned out, she needs workload management. If they're underpaid, she needs compensation adjustments.

### 8. Proactive Management Beats Reactive Crisis Response
**Core Learning:** The best retention plan is excellent day-to-day management. By the time you're "developing a retention plan," you're playing catch-up.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Great managers have ongoing career conversations (quarterly or more)
- Great managers know what motivates each team member
- Great managers create growth opportunities proactively
- Great managers build relationships where honesty is normal

**Key Insight:** If Kathryn had been doing these things, issues would have surfaced early: "Hey Kathryn, I'm feeling stuck in my growth" is much easier to address than "I'm interviewing at other companies."

## Facilitation Overview

### Session Structure (60-75 minutes)
1. Introduction (5 min)
2. Understanding the Challenge (10 min)
3. Investigation Approaches (15 min)
4. Prevention and Proactive Management (15-20 min)
5. Response Strategy (10 min)
6. Synthesis and Takeaways (10 min)

---

## Opening the Discussion

**Suggested Opening:**
"Kathryn faces a challenge many managers encounter: anonymous feedback that something is wrong, but not enough information to know what. How does she learn more without violating the anonymity that enabled the feedback?"

**Initial Poll:** "Have you ever been dissatisfied at work but not felt comfortable telling your manager?"

---

## Discussion Flow

### Phase 1: Why Is This Challenging? (10 min)

**Prompt:** "Why is anonymity challenging? What does it prevent Kathryn from knowing?"

**Create Two Columns:**

| What Anonymity Protects | What Anonymity Prevents |
|-------------------------|-------------------------|
| Individual retaliation | Knowing who is unhappy |
| Honest feedback | Understanding individual situations |
| Psychological safety | Targeted interventions |
| Vulnerable sharing | Immediate conversation with affected people |

**Critical Question:** "How should Kathryn approach this? What would respect the intent of anonymity while allowing her to address the problem?"

**Answer to Draw Out:** Focus on understanding themes, not identifying individuals. Create conditions where people can choose to raise concerns if they want.

### Phase 2: Possible Root Causes (10 min)

**Prompt:** "What might cause engineers on a successful team to consider leaving?"

**Brainstorm Categories:**

**Career and Growth:** Lack of advancement, not learning, unclear path, want different work

**Work Environment:** Burnout, overwork, stress, unsustainable pace

**Relationships:** Conflict, don't feel valued, lack of connection with manager, team culture issues

**Organizational:** Compensation below market, company direction concerns, better opportunities elsewhere

**The Work Itself:** No longer interested in domain, work repetitive, want different challenges

**Key Point:** Different root causes require different solutions. Diagnosis before prescription.

### Phase 3: Investigation Approaches (15 min)

**Prompt:** "Let's explore different ways Kathryn could investigate. For each: What information does it provide? What are the risks?"

**Option 1: Share Results + Team Discussion**
- Pros: Transparency builds trust, normalizes conversation, team collaboration
- Cons: Some won't speak up in group, could create anxiety
- How To Do Well: Frame as "How can we improve?" not "Who is leaving?"

**Option 2: Individual 1:1 Conversations**
- Pros: Private setting safer, can tailor to individual, opportunity to strengthen relationship
- Cons: Could feel interrogative, may seem like trying to identify respondents
- How To Do Well: Frame as "How can I support you better?" Ask everyone, not just suspects

**Option 3: Analyze Existing Survey Data**
- Pros: No additional burden, may reveal patterns, can compare to other teams
- Cons: Limited to what was asked, doesn't involve the team
- How To Do Well: Look for correlations, compare to company-wide, identify demographic patterns

**Best Practice:** Kathryn could analyze survey data AND have team discussion AND do thoughtful 1:1s. A comprehensive approach uses multiple methods.

### Phase 4: What Kathryn Should Have Been Doing (15-20 min)

**Prompt:** "What might Kathryn need to have been doing before the survey to be effective in responding now?"

**Build This List:**

**Regular, Meaningful 1:1s:**
- More than project status—include career, satisfaction, challenges
- Create space for concerns and feedback
- Build relationship where honesty is normal

**Career Development:**
- Understand each person's goals and aspirations
- Create development plans and growth opportunities
- Discuss progression regularly

**Recognition and Appreciation:**
- Acknowledge contributions regularly
- Celebrate successes as team
- Ensure people feel valued

**Work-Life Balance:**
- Monitor for burnout signals
- Respect boundaries
- Adjust workload when needed

**Psychological Safety:**
- Respond well to bad news
- Admit own mistakes
- Make it safe to raise concerns

**Critical Discussion:** "If Kathryn had been doing all of this, would she have been surprised by the survey?"

**Answer:** Probably not. These practices create conditions where people raise concerns early before they become retention risks.

### Phase 5: Turning Crisis into Learning (10 min)

**Prompt:** "How could Kathryn's response actually strengthen the team?"

**Potential Positive Outcomes:**
- Demonstrates that feedback matters and leadership responds
- Opportunity to improve team practices everyone benefits from
- Strengthens relationships through honest conversation
- Addresses festering issues
- Creates more psychological safety if done well

**How to Achieve:** Be transparent, invite team collaboration, take action on feedback, check in regularly on improvements, model vulnerability and learning mindset.

**Example Framing:** "The survey told us we have work to do as a team. I'm grateful for that feedback. Let's figure this out together. What would make this the best team you've ever been on?"

## Key Teaching Points

✓ Retention is proactive, not reactive
✓ Psychological safety enables honest feedback
✓ Respect anonymity, focus on themes
✓ Diagnosis before prescription
✓ Transparency builds trust
✓ Turn crisis into opportunity

---

## Handling Difficult Moments

**If Students Suggest Identifying Who Responded:**
"What happens to trust if people believe their anonymous feedback wasn't really anonymous? What happens to future survey participation?"

**If Students Minimize the Concern ("Only a few people"):**
"On a team that created a 10x improvement framework, losing even one key person might be significant. What's the real cost of turnover?"

**If Discussion Focuses Only on Tactics, Not Root Causes:**
"I'm hearing ideas like raises, bonuses, perks. But what if the root cause is different—like lack of growth or burnout? What happens then?"

---

## Debrief

**Personal Application:** "How often do you have career conversations? Are they deep enough that concerns surface early? What would need to change?"

**Key Takeaways:** "What's most important about retention from this discussion?"

---

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- Treating anonymity as a problem to solve
- Jumping to solutions without diagnosis
- Focusing only on response, not prevention
- Generic retention tactics
- Assuming all retention issues are the same
- Underestimating impact of losing "just a few"

---

*Remember: Your role as facilitator is to guide discovery, not to provide "the answer." The best learning comes from students wrestling with complexity and articulating their own insights.*
