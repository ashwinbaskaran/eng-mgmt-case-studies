# Michael Case Study: Coach Guide

## Key Management Learnings

This section outlines the critical management concepts and skills that students should develop through discussion of this case study.

### 1. **High Morale Without Progress Signals Systemic Issues**

**Core Learning:** Morale measures how people feel, not whether they're effective. High morale despite poor results suggests the team isn't confronting hard truths.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Morale (belief in goals, positive feelings) is different from effectiveness
- Teams can feel good while building the wrong thing or working inefficiently
- High morale + low progress often indicates avoidance of difficult issues
- Feel-good management can mask serious problems

**Key Insight:** PeopleOps found "high morale" despite the team being "weeks in" with "not a lot of progress." This is a red flag, not a reassuring sign. It suggests the team either doesn't recognize they're behind (lack of accountability) or knows but isn't addressing it (conflict avoidance).

---

### 2. **Productive Conflict Is Essential for Effective Teams**

**Core Learning:** Lack of conflict isn't a sign of health—it's often a sign of dysfunction. Great teams debate ideas vigorously while maintaining relationships.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Productive conflict: Debating ideas, challenging assumptions, surfacing problems
- Destructive conflict: Personal attacks, power struggles, ongoing grudges
- Lack of conflict: Artificial harmony, issues swept under rug, groupthink
- Acme has "few, if any, conflicts on serious issues"—this is a warning sign, not a strength

**Key Insight:** The team is "collegial" with "strong opinions" but not making progress. This suggests people have different views but aren't debating them. Instead, they're being nice, deferring, seeking consensus endlessly, or avoiding decisions. This "false harmony" feels good but produces terrible results.

---

### 3. **Decision-Making Effectiveness Trumps Decision-Making Comfort**

**Core Learning:** Some managers optimize for everyone feeling good about decisions rather than making effective decisions quickly. This is a costly mistake.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Consensus feels nice but can be slow and lead to watered-down decisions
- Not all decisions require consensus; some need a clear decision-maker
- "Everyone agrees" can mean "nobody strongly believes in this"
- Senior teams with strong opinions need clear decision frameworks

**Key Insight:** Michael likely wants everyone to feel heard and bought-in (aligned with her excellent 360 feedback). With a senior team that has "strong opinions," this might mean endless discussion, revisiting decisions, or compromised solutions that satisfy no one. The project is falling behind because decisions aren't getting made or implemented decisively.

---

### 4. **Strong 360 Feedback Can Indicate Optimization for the Wrong Things**

**Core Learning:** Managers who score highly on "being liked" may be avoiding the difficult conversations and decisions that drive results.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Michael's 360 feedback highlights relationship skills: understanding goals, advocating, giving credit
- These are important, but not sufficient
- Missing from 360: driving results, making tough calls, holding team accountable
- You can have excellent 360s while team underperforms if you avoid conflict

**Key Insight:** The case explicitly asks: "Are there ways in which strong 360 feedback could be misleading?" Yes—if Michael is optimizing for being liked and avoiding anything that creates tension, she'll score well on interpersonal dimensions while the team struggles. Great managers balance care for people with accountability for results.

---

### 5. **Senior Teams Need Different Management Than Junior Teams**

**Core Learning:** Senior engineers need less direction but potentially more decision-making structure. Strong opinions require clear processes for resolution.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Senior engineers are independent, capable, experienced
- They don't need micromanagement or hand-holding
- They DO need clear decision frameworks when they disagree
- "Let the team figure it out" can lead to gridlock when opinions differ

**Key Insight:** The backoffice team is "senior" with "strong opinions." This is a strength (expertise, experience) and a potential weakness (harder to align, longer debates). Michael needs to provide structure for decision-making: "Here's how we'll make this decision. Here's who has ultimate authority. Here's the timeline."

---

### 6. **Effort Is Not the Same as Results**

**Core Learning:** Teams can be working extremely hard while producing little value if effort is misdirected, decisions keep changing, or work is rework.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- "Hard-working" and "motivated" don't guarantee progress
- Effort without clear direction wastes energy
- Thrashing (starting, stopping, changing direction) creates high effort, low output
- Measuring activity misses whether activity is productive

**Key Insight:** The team is "hard-working" and "motivated" but "not making a lot of progress." They're likely spinning: debating endlessly, revisiting decisions, building and rebuilding, or working on wrong things. Michael needs to diagnose where effort is going and whether it's productive.

---

### 7. **New Managers Face Credibility Challenges with Established Teams**

**Core Learning:** Taking over a successful team as a new manager creates pressure to maintain harmony rather than challenge the status quo, even when change is needed.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Michael joined 6 months ago; team has longer history and successful track record
- Previous manager left "on good terms"—team may compare Michael unfavorably
- Michael may hesitate to make changes or challenge team for fear of losing credibility
- New managers often try to earn trust by being accommodating, which can backfire

**Key Insight:** Michael may be in "don't rock the boat" mode. The team was successful before; Michael is new; easier to be accommodating than directive. But the project needs different approach. Michael must build credibility through results, not just by being liked.

---

### 8. **Adding People Doesn't Fix Process or Decision-Making Problems**

**Core Learning:** "Throw more people at it" is a common but often wrong response to productivity issues. If the problem is how the team works, more people makes it worse.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Adding engineers to late project often makes it later (Brooks's Law)
- If problem is decision-making, more people means more opinions, slower decisions
- If problem is unclear direction, more people means more wasted effort
- Must diagnose root cause before changing team composition

**Key Insight:** The case asks about adding or exchanging engineers. This might help if the problem is skill gaps or insufficient capacity. But if the problem is decision-making dysfunction or lack of productive conflict, it won't help and might hurt. Diagnose first, then decide.

---

## Facilitation Overview

### Session Structure (Recommended: 60-75 minutes)

1. **Introduction** (5 min)
   - Frame the case and learning objectives
   - Set discussion norms

2. **Understanding the Puzzle** (10-15 min)
   - High morale + high effort + low progress = what?
   - Generate hypotheses

3. **The Role of Conflict** (15 min)
   - When is lack of conflict bad?
   - Productive vs. false harmony

4. **Michael's 360 and Management Style** (10-15 min)
   - Strengths and potential blind spots
   - What might Michael be avoiding?

5. **Diagnosis and Solutions** (15 min)
   - How to investigate
   - What actions might help

6. **Synthesis and Takeaways** (10 min)
   - Key learnings
   - Application to students' contexts

---

## Facilitation Guide

### Opening the Discussion

**Goal:** Surface the paradox and engage students' curiosity

**Suggested Opening:**
> "This case presents a puzzle. A senior, hard-working, highly motivated team with high morale is falling behind on a key project. Their new manager has excellent 360 feedback. What's going on?"

**Initial Brainstorm:**
- "What are possible explanations for this situation?"
- "What would you want to know to understand what's happening?"

*Capture all ideas without judgment. Build a list of hypotheses to explore.*

---

### Discussion Flow

#### Phase 1: Understanding the Puzzle (10-15 min)

**Prompt:** "Let's start by really understanding what we're told and what it means."

**Build This Analysis Together:**

**What We Know:**
- Several weeks in, not a lot of progress
- Team is senior, well-regarded, historically successful
- Team is hard-working and highly motivated
- Morale is high
- Michael is new (6 months), has excellent 360 feedback
- Team has "strong opinions," "collegial environment"
- Acme has "few conflicts on serious issues"

**What's Puzzling:**
- High effort + high motivation should = good progress
- High morale despite being behind is unusual
- Senior, capable team shouldn't struggle with backoffice migration
- Michael's excellent people skills should help

**Guided Questions:**
- "What's the difference between morale and productivity?"
- "Can you work hard and not make progress? How?"
- "What does it mean that morale is high even though they're behind?"

**Key Point to Surface:**
Morale measures "Do people believe in the goal and feel positive?" not "Are we being effective?" High morale while behind suggests either:
1. Team doesn't recognize they're behind (lack of accountability/transparency)
2. Team knows but isn't addressing root causes (conflict avoidance)

Either one is a problem.

---

#### Phase 2: Hypothesis Generation (10 min)

**Prompt:** "Let's generate hypotheses. What could explain low productivity despite high effort and morale?"

**Facilitate Brainstorm (Capture All):**

**Category 1: Technical**
- Payment provider migration is harder than expected
- Architecture decisions need revisiting
- Technical debt slowing everything down
- Integration complexity underestimated

**Category 2: Decision-Making**
- Decisions taking too long
- Too much consensus-seeking with "strong opinions"
- Decisions getting revisited constantly
- No clear decision-maker or framework

**Category 3: Process**
- Too many meetings, not enough execution time
- Unclear responsibilities or ownership
- Inefficient workflows
- Too much coordination overhead

**Category 4: Requirements/Scope**
- Scope keeps changing
- Requirements unclear
- Building wrong thing
- Stakeholder misalignment

**Category 5: People/Culture**
- Conflict avoidance ("collegial" = too nice)
- False harmony (appear to agree but don't commit)
- New manager hesitant to be directive
- Team resistant to changes from new manager

**Discussion:**
> "Which of these would explain high morale despite being behind? Which wouldn't?"

**Answer to Draw Out:** Technical challenges would likely frustrate people (lower morale). Decision-making dysfunction or conflict avoidance could coexist with high morale—people feel good because there's no tension, even though nothing gets decided or done.

---

#### Phase 3: The Conflict Question (15 min)

**Prompt:** "The case mentions Acme has 'few, if any, conflicts on serious issues' and asks 'In what ways can lack of conflict be a bad thing?' Let's explore this."

**Create Three Columns:**

| **Productive Conflict** | **Destructive Conflict** | **No Conflict (False Harmony)** |
|------------------------|-------------------------|--------------------------------|
| Debate ideas vigorously | Personal attacks | Avoid disagreement |
| Challenge assumptions | Power struggles | Defer to avoid tension |
| Surface problems early | Ongoing grudges | Issues swept under rug |
| Commit after debate | Win/lose mentality | Appear to agree, don't commit |
| Results: Better decisions | Results: Damaged relationships | Results: Slow/poor decisions |

**Guided Questions:**
- "When is lack of conflict a good sign? When is it a warning sign?"
- "What might 'collegial environment' mean in practice?"
- "How do you have strong opinions AND be collegial? What does that require?"

**Critical Discussion:**
> "The team has 'strong opinions' and is 'collegial.' If people have different strong opinions, how do decisions get made in a collegial environment?"

**Possible Answers:**
- Long discussions trying to convince everyone (slow)
- Compromise solutions that satisfy no one (weak)
- Deference to avoid conflict (groupthink)
- Decisions get revisited because people didn't really buy in (churn)

**Key Teaching Point:**
> "Healthy teams have vigorous debate followed by commitment. They argue about ideas, then align behind decisions. Unhealthy teams either avoid conflict (false harmony) or have conflict that damages relationships. Which do you think is happening here?"

**Answer to Draw Out:** Likely false harmony. People are being "collegial" by not pushing their strong opinions, leading to slow decisions or weak compromises.

---

#### Phase 4: Michael's 360 Feedback Analysis (10-15 min)

**Prompt:** "Michael has excellent 360 feedback. Let's look at what's highlighted: understanding career objectives, advocating for team members, giving credit. These are real strengths. But the case asks: Could strong 360 feedback be misleading? How?"

**Facilitate Building This Analysis:**

**What 360 Shows Michael Does Well:**
- Understands people's career goals
- Advocates for them
- Gives them credit
- People feel supported and valued

**What Might Be Missing:**
- Driving results and accountability
- Making tough decisions quickly
- Holding people accountable for outcomes
- Having difficult conversations
- Pushing back or disagreeing with team

**Discussion Questions:**
- "Could Michael be optimizing for being liked rather than being effective?"
- "What might Michael be avoiding to maintain excellent 360s?"
- "If you're a new manager taking over a successful team, what are you nervous about?"

**Key Insight to Surface:**
Michael might be in "don't rock the boat" mode. Being new to a successful team, she may be focused on building relationships and being well-liked rather than driving hard conversations about results. Her 360 suggests she's succeeding at being supportive but may not be providing the directive leadership the project needs.

**The Shadow Side of Strengths:**
- Understanding career goals → Overemphasis on individual happiness vs. team results
- Advocating for team → Not holding team accountable
- Giving credit → Avoiding tough feedback
- Being collegial → Avoiding necessary conflict

---

#### Phase 5: Diagnostic Questions (10 min)

**Prompt:** "If you're Michael, what do you need to understand? What questions should you ask? What should you investigate?"

**Build This List Together:**

**Questions for the Team (In Retro or 1:1s):**
- "What's blocking our progress?"
- "When we make decisions, what happens? Do they stick or get revisited?"
- "Are there technical challenges we're not addressing?"
- "Do we have clear ownership of decisions and work?"
- "What would need to change for us to move faster?"

**Questions for Stakeholders:**
- "Are requirements clear and stable?"
- "What's the real deadline and flexibility?"
- "How do you perceive the team's progress?"

**Things to Observe:**
- How long does it take to make decisions?
- Do decisions stick or get revisited?
- How does the team handle disagreement?
- Where is time actually going?
- Are there patterns in what's blocked vs. what moves forward?

**Key Point:**
Michael needs to be diagnostic, not assumptive. Investigate before prescribing solutions.

---

#### Phase 6: Solutions Exploration (15 min)

**Prompt:** "Based on what we think might be happening, what should Michael do?"

**If the Problem Is Decision-Making Dysfunction:**
- Establish clear decision-making framework (DACI, RACI, etc.)
- Define who makes what decisions
- Set time limits for decisions
- "Disagree and commit" norm
- Make decision-making process explicit

**If the Problem Is Lack of Productive Conflict:**
- Model healthy disagreement
- Explicitly invite dissent
- Reward people who surface problems
- Separate idea debate from personal relationships
- Teach productive conflict skills

**If the Problem Is Michael's Leadership Style:**
- Shift from accommodating to directive when needed
- Balance relationship-building with results accountability
- Have direct conversation about being behind
- Set clearer expectations and deadlines
- Be willing to make unpopular decisions

**If the Problem Is Technical:**
- Bring in outside expertise for second opinion
- Revisit architecture decisions
- Address technical debt blocking progress
- Adjust timeline or scope based on reality

**The Multi-Pronged Approach:**
> "Notice these aren't mutually exclusive. Michael likely needs to: (1) diagnose the root cause, (2) have an honest conversation with the team about being behind, (3) establish clearer decision-making processes, and (4) model more directive leadership while maintaining support for the team."

**The Adding People Question:**
"The case asks about adding or exchanging engineers. When would that help? When wouldn't it?"

**Answer:** Adding people helps if the problem is insufficient capacity or specific skill gaps. It doesn't help (and often hurts) if the problem is decision-making, process, or direction. Diagnose before acting.

---

### Handling Difficult Moments

#### If Students Blame Michael Harshly

**Intervention:**
> "It's easy to see Michael's mistakes from the outside. But imagine you're new to a successful team. You want to build relationships and trust. Your instinct might be to be supportive and accommodating. How does a manager balance being supportive with being directive?"

#### If Students Miss the Conflict Angle

**Intervention:**
> "I notice we haven't talked much about Acme's culture of 'few conflicts' or the team being 'collegial.' Why might the case include those details? What might they tell us?"

*Guide toward recognizing conflict avoidance as core issue.*

#### If Students Focus Only on Technical Explanations

**Intervention:**
> "Technical challenges could certainly explain delays. But would they explain high morale? If the team was wrestling with hard technical problems, would PeopleOps find everyone feeling great? What does that mismatch tell us?"

#### If Discussion Stays Abstract

**Intervention:**
> "Let's get concrete. You're Michael. Monday morning. You're behind on the project. The team feels fine. What specifically do you do?"

## Key Teaching Points to Ensure Coverage

Make sure these concepts emerge during discussion:

### ✓ **Productive conflict is essential**
- Lack of conflict often means avoidance, not alignment
- Healthy teams debate ideas vigorously
- False harmony produces poor results

### ✓ **Morale ≠ Effectiveness**
- Teams can feel good while being unproductive
- High morale despite poor results is red flag
- Must measure results, not just sentiment

### ✓ **Decision-making effectiveness matters**
- Consensus can be too slow
- Clear decision frameworks help senior teams
- "Disagree and commit" beats endless discussion

### ✓ **Strong 360s can be misleading**
- Can optimize for being liked vs. being effective
- Relationship skills necessary but not sufficient
- Must balance support with accountability

### ✓ **Senior teams need structure too**
- Independence doesn't mean no process
- Strong opinions require decision frameworks
- Different management, not less management

### ✓ **Adding people isn't always the answer**
- Process problems get worse with more people
- Diagnose root cause first
- Brooks's Law: adding people to late project makes it later

## Debrief and Synthesis (10 min)

### Closing Questions

**Personal Application:**
> "Think about your own team or teams you've been on. Is there productive conflict? How do you know? What's the difference between productive and destructive conflict?"

**Key Takeaways:**
> "What's the most important thing you learned from this case about team effectiveness?"

**Commitment to Action:**
> "What's one thing you'll do differently to ensure your team has productive conflict and effective decision-making?"

---

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

### ❌ **Assuming Lack of Conflict Is Good**
It's often a warning sign. Push students to think critically.

### ❌ **Missing the Decision-Making Angle**
This is likely the core issue. Don't let it get lost.

### ❌ **Blaming Michael Without Empathy**
Help students understand the pressures new managers face.

### ❌ **Jumping to "Add More People"**
Make students diagnose root cause first.

### ❌ **Treating Morale as Success Metric**
Morale matters, but results matter more. Both are needed.

### ❌ **Not Discussing Senior Team Dynamics**
Senior teams have specific challenges that differ from junior teams.

## Assessment: What Good Discussion Looks Like

Students are learning well if they:

- ✅ Recognize high morale + low progress as potential red flag
- ✅ Understand the importance of productive conflict
- ✅ See the connection to decision-making effectiveness
- ✅ Analyze Michael's 360 feedback critically
- ✅ Distinguish between different types of productivity problems
- ✅ Avoid simple solutions like "add more people"
- ✅ Show empathy for Michael's position while identifying gaps
- ✅ Connect to their own experiences with conflict and decisions

## Follow-Up Activities (Optional)

### Writing Exercise
Ask students to write:
1. The conversation Michael should have with the team about being behind (300 words)
2. A decision-making framework for the backoffice team (200 words)
3. Michael's self-reflection on her management approach (200 words)

### Small Group Work
Divide into groups:
- Group A: Design a team retro to surface real issues
- Group B: Create a decision-making charter for senior teams
- Group C: Role-play: How do you introduce productive conflict?

### Debate Format
Assign positions:
- Team 1: The problem is technical complexity
- Team 2: The problem is decision-making dysfunction
- Team 3: The problem is Michael's leadership style

Each defends their position, then discuss which has the most evidence.
