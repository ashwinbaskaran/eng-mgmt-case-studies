# Sisko Case Study: Coach Guide

## Key Management Learnings

### 1. Manager Roles Must Evolve as Teams Scale
**Core Learning:** What works for managing 6 people doesn't work for 15. Managers must intentionally evolve their role, not just do more of the same.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Sisko is currently a player-coach (manager + lead engineer)
- This model doesn't scale to 15 people
- Trying to keep the same role while team doubles leads to burnout and bottlenecks
- Role evolution is growth, not failure

**Key Insight:** The skills that made Sisko successful at 6 people can become liabilities at 15 if not adapted. Great managers recognize when to change their approach.

### 2. Span of Control Has Real Limits
**Core Learning:** Research shows managers have optimal spans of control. Exceeding them reduces effectiveness for everyone.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Typical effective span: 5-8 direct reports for hands-on management
- Can stretch to 10-12 for very senior, independent teams
- Beyond that, something must change (structure, role, support)

**Key Insight:** Sisko managing 15 people directly means shallow relationships, reactive management, and limited development focus. The team needs substructure or Sisko needs to manage managers.

### 3. Team Structure Should Follow the Work
**Core Learning:** Organizational structure should be designed around actual work patterns, not generic org-chart templates.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Banks team does bespoke integrations, each requiring ongoing maintenance
- Could organize by: customer/bank, technology layer, new vs. maintenance
- Different structures enable different strengths

**Key Insight:** Sisko should ask: "How does work actually flow?" "Where are handoffs?" The answers reveal natural team boundaries.

### 4. Delegation Is a Skill, Not Just Dumping Work
**Core Learning:** Effective delegation means developing others to take ownership, not just assigning tasks.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Sisko can't remain lead engineer while managing 15 people
- Good delegation develops people and creates organizational capacity
- Requires identifying what to delegate, who to delegate to, how to support them

**Key Insight:** Sisko needs to develop other technical leaders through intentional investment, gradual handoff with support, and comfort with different approaches.

### 5. Growth Phases Have Predictable Inflection Points
**Core Learning:** Team scaling isn't linear. Certain sizes trigger qualitative changes in how teams must operate.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- 1-6: Manager can be hands-on, know everything
- 7-12: Need more structure; delegation essential
- 12+: Often requires management layers
- Each inflection requires conscious role adjustment

**Key Insight:** Sisko is hitting a major inflection (6→15). The team will need different structure, communication patterns, and decision-making. Plan proactively, not reactively.

### 6. Player-Coach Model Has an Expiration Date
**Core Learning:** The player-coach model works in specific contexts but doesn't scale indefinitely.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Works well: small teams (3-6), highly experienced teams, stable environments
- Breaks down: larger teams, high-growth phases, teams needing development
- Transition to full-time manager is common and healthy

**Key Insight:** Sisko must decide: transition to full-time management, develop a lead engineer, or stay player-coach with strong team leads. Make the choice consciously, not by drifting.

### 7. Asking for Support Is Strategic Leadership
**Core Learning:** Knowing what support you need and asking for it is a strength, not a weakness.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Sisko has "wide latitude" to shape growth
- Leadership often can't help if they don't know what's needed
- Specific requests are more effective than vague concerns

**Key Insight:** Sisko should think: "What do I need to be successful at 15 people?" Possibilities: training, mentor, recruiting support, budget for team leads, coordination tools.

### 8. Structure Enables or Constrains Performance
**Core Learning:** How you organize the team affects what the team can accomplish. Structure is a strategic choice.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Flat structure: fast decisions, but high coordination overhead
- Hierarchical structure: clear accountability, but slower
- Pod/squad structure: autonomy and ownership, but potential duplication

**Key Insight:** No universally "best" structure. Choose based on the work, the people, and the goals. Revisit as these factors change.

## Facilitation Overview

### Session Structure (60-75 minutes)
1. Introduction (5 min)
2. Current State Analysis (10 min)
3. Growth Patterns Exploration (20 min)
4. Role Evolution (15 min)
5. Inflection Points and Support (10 min)
6. Synthesis and Takeaways (10 min)

---

## Opening the Discussion

**Suggested Opening:**
"Sisko has budget to grow from 6 to 15 people. But growth isn't just addition—it often requires transformation. What changes when a team more than doubles?"

**Initial Question:** "Who's been part of a team that grew significantly? What changed? What got harder? What got better?"

---

## Discussion Flow

### Phase 1: Understanding Current State (10 min)

**Prompt:** "The case says Sisko is 'doubling as the lead engineer.' What does that tell us?"

**Create "Day in the Life" Snapshot:**

**Sisko's Current Role (6 people):**
- Technical work: code, architecture, code review
- People management: 1:1s, hiring, performance
- Project management: planning, customer coordination
- Lead engineer: deep technical decisions

**What Makes This Viable at 6:**
- Can have meaningful 1:1s with everyone
- Can stay close to all technical work
- Direct communication works

**Key Question:** "Now imagine this at 15 people. What breaks?"

**Answers to Surface:**
- 15 × 1 hour biweekly 1:1s = 7.5 hours every two weeks
- Can't review all code or be in all technical decisions
- Too much coordination overhead
- Sisko becomes bottleneck

### Phase 2: Growth Patterns Exploration (20 min)

**Prompt:** "Let's explore different ways to structure a 15-person team. Different patterns have different tradeoffs."

**Option 1: Flat Team (Sisko manages all 15)**
- Pros: Simple, everyone has direct access
- Cons: Sisko spread thin, bottleneck for decisions
- When this works: Very senior, independent team

**Option 2: Tech Leads Model (Sisko manages 15, with 2-3 tech leads)**
- Pros: Technical leadership distributed, develops future leaders
- Cons: Tech leads have unclear authority, may not reduce Sisko's load much
- When this works: Team needs technical leadership; not ready for sub-teams

**Option 3: Team Leads Model (Sisko manages 3 leads, they manage ~4-5 each)**
- Pros: Manageable span, scales beyond 15, clear accountability
- Cons: Adds layer, Sisko loses direct IC relationships
- When this works: Team will keep growing; people ready to manage

**Option 4: Pod/Squad Model (3-4 small teams, each with a lead)**
- Pros: Organized around customers/domains, ownership and autonomy
- Cons: Potential duplication, coordination challenges
- When this works: Work naturally divides; value autonomy over efficiency

**For Each:** "How would this align with Banks team's work? What does this require? What problems does it solve and create?"

**Critical Discussion:** "The question isn't 'which is best' but 'which best fits the work, people, and goals?'"

### Phase 3: Role Evolution Deep Dive (15 min)

**Prompt:** "However Sisko structures the team, his role must change. Let's map that evolution."

**Build From → To Mapping:**

| What Sisko Does Now | What Sisko Should Do at 15 | Who Handles Old Work |
|---------------------|----------------------------|---------------------|
| Lead engineer, deep technical work | Technical strategy and architecture | Tech leads or senior engineers |
| Code review for most code | Review critical pieces, develop reviewers | Distributed to team |
| All customer relationships | Strategic relationships | Team leads or account owners |
| All hiring decisions | Hiring strategy | Team leads participate |
| All 1:1s personally | 1:1s with leads, skip-levels with ICs | Team leads have regular 1:1s |

**Common Challenges:**
- Letting go: Hard to stop doing things you're good at
- Trust: Concern that others won't do it "right"
- Identity: "I'm a great engineer" → "I enable great engineering"

**Key Teaching Point:** "This transition—from doing to enabling—is one of the hardest parts of becoming a senior manager."

### Phase 4: Inflection Points and Timing (10 min)

**Prompt:** "Growth doesn't happen all at once. When does the role need to shift?"

**Build Inflection Timeline:**

**6-8 people:**
- Sisko can still be player-coach
- Start identifying future tech leads
- Begin delegating some technical decisions

**9-12 people:**
- Player-coach model getting strained
- Need clear tech leads or transition to full-time management

**13-15 people:**
- Must have team leads or sub-teams
- Sisko focused on management, strategy, coordination

**Key Insight:** "These changes should be proactive, not reactive. What happens if Sisko waits until he's drowning?"

**Answer:** Crisis management, poor decisions, burnout, team suffers. Better to plan transitions ahead.

### Phase 5: Support Needs (10 min)

**Prompt:** "Sisko has 'wide latitude' in shaping growth. What should he ask for from leadership?"

**Brainstorm Support Needs:**

**Training:** Management training, coach/mentor, leadership development for tech leads

**Resources:** Recruiting support, onboarding program, coordination tools

**Structural:** Budget for team lead roles, approval for subteam structure, clarity on decision rights

**Key Teaching Point:** Effective leaders are specific: "To grow to 15 people while maintaining quality, I need: (1) budget for 2-3 team lead roles, (2) a management coach, and (3) recruiting support for 6 months."

---

## Key Teaching Points

✓ Scaling requires role evolution
✓ Span of control matters
✓ Structure follows work
✓ Delegation is skill
✓ Inflection points are predictable
✓ Asking for support is leadership

---

## Handling Difficult Moments

**If Students Suggest Sisko Can Stay Player-Coach:**
"Let's do the math. Fifteen 1:1s biweekly = how many hours? Plus hiring, reviews, planning... Can Sisko also be lead engineer? What gets sacrificed?"

**If Discussion Stays Too Abstract:**
"Let's get concrete. Tomorrow Sisko has 10 people, next month 13. What specifically changes in his day-to-day? What does he stop doing?"

**If Students Don't Address the People Side:**
"We've talked org charts. But what about the humans? How does Sisko help his current team through this change?"

---

## Debrief

**Personal Application:** "As your team or scope grows, how does your role need to evolve? What might you need to let go of?"

**Key Takeaways:** "What's most important about scaling teams from this discussion?"

---

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- Assuming growth is just addition
- Treating all structures as equally valid
- Ignoring the emotional/identity challenge
- Forgetting about existing team members
- Not considering the work itself
- Skipping the details of transition

---

*Remember: Your role as facilitator is to guide discovery, not to provide "the answer." The best learning comes from students wrestling with complexity and articulating their own insights.*
