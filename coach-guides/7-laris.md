# Laris Case Study: Coach Guide

## Key Management Learnings

### 1. Hiring Gridlock Often Signals Deeper Disagreements
**Core Learning:** When smart people can't agree on hiring after multiple candidates, the problem usually isn't the candidates—it's misalignment on strategy, role definition, or authority.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- 8 weeks and 5 candidates with no hire is not normal
- "Unanimous approval" requirement can mask underlying conflicts
- People use hiring process to fight proxy battles about strategy
- Hiring disagreements often indicate technical or organizational disagreements

**Key Insight:** Agnes, Elnor, and Jack likely disagree about something fundamental—the modular approach, who should lead it, whether a new sub-team is needed. Rather than debate directly, they're expressing disagreement through hiring vetos. Laris needs to surface the real conflict.

### 2. Hiring Bars Must Be Calibrated and Reality-Tested
**Core Learning:** "Hire the best" sounds good but can mean "hire nobody." Hiring bars must balance quality with feasibility and urgency.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Perfect candidates often don't exist or aren't available
- Comparing candidates to yourself creates unrealistic bar
- Context matters: startup vs. mature company, timeline, availability
- "Very rigorous screening" can be code for impossibly high bar

**Key Insight:** Agnes screens "very rigorously" but can't hire anyone. This suggests either: (1) the bar is unrealistically high, (2) the role is poorly defined, or (3) Agnes is looking for a clone of herself.

### 3. Unanimous Approval Requirements Can Paralyze Organizations
**Core Learning:** Consensus-based decision-making gives everyone veto power, which can prevent any decision. Clear decision-makers and frameworks are essential.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Unanimous approval means any single person can block
- Works well for small decisions or very aligned teams
- Fails for complex decisions or when misalignment exists
- Creates passive-aggressive power dynamics

**Key Insight:** The hiring process requires "unanimous approval." If there's underlying disagreement about strategy, each candidate becomes a referendum on that disagreement. Better: clear hiring manager with final decision authority.

### 4. The Roving Expert Pattern Has Hidden Costs
**Core Learning:** Sending senior engineers to "fix" struggling teams can work short-term but creates dependency, resentment, and organizational learning problems.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Agnes has been moved multiple times to teams with "thorniest challenges"
- Leadership "eagerly" gives her what she asks for
- This pattern might signal leadership doesn't trust teams or teams don't develop capability
- Can create resentment and resistance

**Key Insight:** Elnor and Jack are founding Starfleet members. Agnes arrives, spends 2 weeks, then recommends major changes. Leadership immediately agrees. How might Elnor and Jack feel? Possibly undermined or resentful.

### 5. Technical Disagreements Disguise as Process Disagreements
**Core Learning:** When teams can't agree on hiring, processes, or org structure, there's often an underlying technical disagreement they haven't resolved.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Agnes recommended "more modular approach"
- Did Elnor and Jack agree? Were they consulted?
- If they disagree with the approach, they might resist hiring for it
- Process conflicts often mask technical or strategic conflicts

**Key Insight:** The case doesn't say whether Elnor and Jack agreed with Agnes's modular approach. If they didn't, they might be using hiring as passive resistance: "We can't find the right person" really means "We don't agree this role should exist."

### 6. Authority and Decision Rights Must Be Explicit
**Core Learning:** When it's unclear who has authority to make decisions, organizations get stuck in endless consensus-seeking or political maneuvering.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Who decided on the modular approach? Agnes proposed, leadership agreed, but what about the team?
- Who has authority to make hiring decisions? Nominally Agnes but committee has veto
- Unclear authority creates conflict and delay
- Explicit decision frameworks prevent gridlock

**Key Insight:** Laris needs to clarify: Who owns the technical strategy for Starfleet? Who has final say on hiring? If Agnes is creating a sub-team, she should probably have hiring authority with input from others.

### 7. Crisis Response Patterns Reveal Organizational Health
**Core Learning:** How an organization responds to crises shows its underlying health. Repeatedly sending the same person to fix problems prevents organizational learning.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Agnes has been moved to many struggling teams
- Pattern: identify crisis → send Agnes → eagerly support her
- This prevents teams from developing problem-solving capability
- Creates single point of failure (Agnes)
- Doesn't build organizational resilience

**Key Insight:** Starfleet was "running up against a wall" so leadership moved Agnes there. Better: Agnes coaches teams to solve problems themselves, or systemic issues get addressed so teams don't hit walls.

### 8. New Managers Need to Investigate, Not Assume
**Core Learning:** When taking over a struggling situation, effective managers gather data from multiple perspectives before acting.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Laris is new and needs to build understanding
- Different stakeholders have different perspectives
- Must understand both stated and unstated issues
- Investigation should inform action

**Key Insight:** The case asks "Where should Laris dig?" Laris should: talk to Agnes, Elnor, Jack separately; understand hiring process and disagreements; investigate technical strategy alignment; understand history and dynamics.

## Facilitation Overview

### Session Structure (60-75 minutes)
1. Introduction (5 min)
2. Mapping the Situation (10 min)
3. Stakeholder Perspectives (15 min)
4. Root Cause Hypotheses (15 min)
5. Decision Analysis and Patterns (10 min)
6. Laris's Path Forward (10 min)
7. Synthesis and Takeaways (10 min)

---

## Opening the Discussion

**Suggested Opening:**
"Laris is new to this team and encounters a puzzling situation: a critical project, a hiring process that's stuck, senior engineers who can't agree, and complex history. Let's start by mapping what we know."

**Initial Mapping:** List key players and relationships on board.

---

## Discussion Flow

### Phase 1: Understanding the Puzzle (10 min)

**Prompt:** "What stands out to you as puzzling here?"

**Capture Student Observations:**
- 8 weeks, 5 candidates, no hire on critical project
- Agnes screens "very rigorously"
- No "unanimous approval"
- Leadership "eagerly" supports Agnes
- Agnes proposed modular approach quickly after arriving
- Elnor and Jack are on hiring committee

**Key Question:** "What do we not know that we'd want to know?"

**Important Missing Information:**
- Did Elnor and Jack agree with the modular approach?
- What specific feedback is given on candidates?
- Where do evaluators disagree?
- What does "very rigorous" screening mean?
- What's the relationship quality between Agnes, Elnor, and Jack?

### Phase 2: Stakeholder Perspectives (15 min)

**Prompt:** "The case asks us to consider how each stakeholder might be contributing. Let's go through each."

**Agnes:**
- Contributing to problem: Possibly unrealistic bar, proposed approach without full buy-in, pattern of changing things
- Contributing to solution: Deep expertise, can articulate what's needed, has authority from leadership

**Elnor:**
- Contributing to problem: Might disagree with modular approach, could be protecting original vision, using hiring as veto
- Contributing to solution: Founding context, could help calibrate bar, could voice concerns directly

**Jack:**
- Contributing to problem: Might disagree with Agnes, referred Laris with unclear expectations
- Contributing to solution: Relationship with Laris, might be bridge between parties, historical context

**Laris:**
- Contributing to problem: New, may not have full context, referred by Jack (possible perceived bias)
- Contributing to solution: Fresh perspective, can investigate without history, authority as manager

**Leadership:**
- Contributing to problem: Pattern of using Agnes as fix-all, may give Agnes too much authority too quickly
- Contributing to solution: Can clarify authority and priorities, can support alignment process

**Discussion:** "Notice how many are about misalignment, authority, and trust—not about candidate quality. What does that tell us?"

### Phase 3: Root Cause Hypotheses (15 min)

**Prompt:** "The case asks us to consider people, technical, and process issues. Let's build hypotheses in each category."

**People Issues:**
- Disagreement about technical approach (Agnes vs. Elnor/Jack)
- Trust deficit from roving engineer pattern
- Resentment about Agnes getting resources
- Passive resistance through hiring
- Power dynamics unclear

**Technical Issues:**
- Modular approach not well-defined
- Disagreement about architecture
- Role requirements unclear
- Different technical philosophies
- Uncertainty about what's needed

**Process Issues:**
- Unanimous approval gives everyone veto
- No clear hiring manager/decision-maker
- Hiring bar not calibrated
- Too rigorous screening
- Feedback not actionable

**Critical Question:** "If you had to guess the primary issue, what would it be?"

**Guide Toward:** Most likely a combination: disagreement about technical approach that's unexpressed and manifesting as hiring gridlock. The unanimous approval requirement enables passive resistance.

### Phase 4: Decision Analysis and Patterns (10 min)

**Prompt:** "Let's look at key decisions and the roving engineer pattern."

**Key Decisions:**

**Leadership Moving Agnes to Starfleet:**
- Tradeoffs: Brings expertise but may undermine existing team

**Agnes Proposing Modular Approach:**
- Tradeoffs: Fresh perspective but only 2 weeks of context

**Creating New Sub-team and Budget:**
- Tradeoffs: Resources to execute but quick agreement might bypass healthy debate

**Unanimous Approval for Hiring:**
- Tradeoffs: Ensures everyone comfortable but gives everyone veto power

**The Roving Engineer Pattern:**

**Benefits:**
- Expertise brought to struggling teams
- Cross-pollination of ideas
- Crisis resolution

**Costs/Risks:**
- Teams don't develop own capability
- Dependence on single person
- Can create resentment
- Parachuting in might miss context

**Discussion:** "When is this pattern helpful? When harmful?"

### Phase 5: Where Should Laris Dig? (10-15 min)

**Prompt:** "How can Laris figure out what's going on? Where should she dig?"

**Build Investigation Plan:**

**Week 1: Individual Conversations**
- Agnes: What are you looking for? What feedback on candidates? What's the modular approach and why? How's working with Elnor and Jack?
- Elnor: Your perspective on Starfleet progress? On modular approach? On candidates? What do you need?
- Jack: Same questions. Plus: Why did you refer Laris?
- Less senior engineers: How's the project going? What's working? What's not?

**What to Listen For:**
- Alignment or misalignment on technical approach
- Who agrees/disagrees with what
- Stated vs. unstated concerns
- Relationship quality and trust

**Week 1-2: Process Investigation**
- Review hiring feedback on all 5 candidates
- Understand where evaluators disagreed
- Examine hiring bar and role definition
- Assess whether unanimous approval is right process

**Week 2: Facilitated Alignment**
- Bring Agnes, Elnor, Jack together
- Surface technical disagreements
- Discuss modular approach explicitly
- Align on role needs and hiring bar
- Agree on decision-making process

**Critical Questions:**
1. Is everyone aligned on the technical approach?
2. Is the role well-defined and realistic?
3. Is the hiring bar calibrated to market and urgency?
4. Is the decision-making process working?
5. Are there trust or relationship issues to address?

## Key Teaching Points

✓ Hiring gridlock signals deeper problems
✓ Hiring bars must be calibrated
✓ Unanimous approval can paralyze
✓ Roving expert pattern has costs
✓ Technical disagreements disguise as process issues
✓ Authority must be clear
✓ New managers should investigate deeply

---

## Handling Difficult Moments

**If Students Focus Only on Hiring Process:**
"Why would three smart engineers struggle to agree on five candidates? What might that signal about something deeper?"

**If Students Blame Agnes:**
"Agnes has a track record. Leadership trusts her. But we're seeing resistance. Is Agnes the problem, or is something about how she's being used creating issues?"

**If Students Miss the Roving Engineer Pattern:**
"The case gives us Agnes's history: protocol group, payments team, econometrics, now Starfleet. She's been moved many times. Why might that matter?"

---

## Debrief

**Personal Application:** "Have you seen hiring processes get stuck? What was really going on beneath the surface?"

**Key Takeaways:** "What's most important about organizational dynamics from this case?"

---

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- Treating this as only a hiring problem
- Missing the roving engineer pattern
- Blaming individuals vs. seeing system
- Not examining each stakeholder's perspective
- Accepting unanimous approval as reasonable
- Skipping the decision analysis

---

*Remember: Your role as facilitator is to guide discovery, not to provide "the answer." The best learning comes from students wrestling with complexity and articulating their own insights.*
