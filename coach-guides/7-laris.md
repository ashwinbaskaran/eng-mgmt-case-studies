# Laris Case Study: Coach Guide

## Key Management Learnings

This section outlines the critical management concepts and skills that students should develop through discussion of this case study.

### 1. **Hiring Gridlock Often Signals Deeper Disagreements**

**Core Learning:** When smart people can't agree on hiring after multiple candidates, the problem usually isn't the candidates—it's misalignment on strategy, role definition, or authority.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- 8 weeks and 5 candidates with no hire is not normal
- "Unanimous approval" requirement can mask underlying conflicts
- People use hiring process to fight proxy battles about strategy
- Hiring disagreements often indicate technical or organizational disagreements

**Key Insight:** The hiring difficulty is a symptom, not the disease. Agnes, Elnor, and Jack likely disagree about something fundamental—the modular approach, who should lead it, whether a new sub-team is needed, or Agnes's role. Rather than debate directly, they're expressing disagreement through hiring vetos. Laris needs to surface the real conflict.

---

### 2. **Hiring Bars Must Be Calibrated and Reality-Tested**

**Core Learning:** "Hire the best" sounds good but can mean "hire nobody." Hiring bars must balance quality with feasibility and urgency.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Perfect candidates often don't exist or aren't available
- Comparing candidates to yourself creates unrealistic bar
- Context matters: startup vs. mature company, timeline, availability
- "Very rigorous screening" can be code for impossibly high bar

**Key Insight:** Agnes screens "very rigorously" but can't hire anyone. This suggests either: (1) the bar is unrealistically high, (2) the role is poorly defined, or (3) Agnes is looking for a clone of herself. The hiring committee needs to calibrate: What does this role actually need? What's realistic given market, timeline, and compensation? What's good enough to move forward?

---

### 3. **Unanimous Approval Requirements Can Paralyze Organizations**

**Core Learning:** Consensus-based decision-making gives everyone veto power, which can prevent any decision. Clear decision-makers and frameworks are essential.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Unanimous approval means any single person can block
- Works well for small decisions or very aligned teams
- Fails for complex decisions or when misalignment exists
- Creates passive-aggressive power dynamics

**Key Insight:** The hiring process requires "unanimous approval" from the committee. This means Agnes, Elnor, or Jack can individually veto. If there's underlying disagreement about strategy or approach, each candidate becomes a referendum on that disagreement. Better process: clear hiring manager with final decision authority, others provide input but don't have veto.

---

### 4. **The Roving Expert Pattern Has Hidden Costs**

**Core Learning:** Sending senior engineers to "fix" struggling teams can work short-term but creates dependency, resentment, and organizational learning problems.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Agnes has been moved multiple times to teams with "thorniest challenges"
- Leadership "eagerly" gives her what she asks for
- This pattern might signal: leadership doesn't trust teams, teams don't develop capability, Agnes gets special treatment
- Can create resentment and resistance

**Key Insight:** Elnor and Jack are founding Starfleet members. Agnes arrives, spends 2 weeks, then recommends major changes (modular approach) and asks for new team/budget. Leadership immediately agrees. How might Elnor and Jack feel? Possibly undermined, distrusted, or resentful. Their hiring resistance might be passive resistance to Agnes's approach or authority.

---

### 5. **Technical Disagreements Disguise as Process Disagreements**

**Core Learning:** When teams can't agree on hiring, processes, or org structure, there's often an underlying technical disagreement they haven't resolved.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Agnes recommended "more modular approach"
- Did Elnor and Jack agree? Were they consulted?
- If they disagree with the approach, they might resist hiring for it
- Process conflicts often mask technical or strategic conflicts

**Key Insight:** The case doesn't say whether Elnor and Jack agreed with Agnes's modular approach. If they didn't, they might be using hiring as passive resistance: "We can't find the right person" really means "We don't agree this role should exist." Laris needs to surface whether there's alignment on the technical strategy before solving the hiring problem.

---

### 6. **Authority and Decision Rights Must Be Explicit**

**Core Learning:** When it's unclear who has authority to make decisions, organizations get stuck in endless consensus-seeking or political maneuvering.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Who decided on the modular approach? Agnes proposed, leadership agreed, but what about the team?
- Who has authority to make hiring decisions? Nominally Agnes (creating her sub-team) but committee has veto
- Unclear authority creates conflict and delay
- Explicit decision frameworks prevent gridlock

**Key Insight:** Laris needs to clarify: Who owns the technical strategy for Starfleet? Who has final say on hiring? If Agnes is creating a sub-team, she should probably have hiring authority with input from others. If the team doesn't trust Agnes's judgment, that's a different problem to solve.

---

### 7. **Crisis Response Patterns Reveal Organizational Health**

**Core Learning:** How an organization responds to crises shows its underlying health. Repeatedly sending the same person to fix problems prevents organizational learning.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Agnes has been moved to many struggling teams
- Leadership pattern: identify crisis → send Agnes → eagerly support her
- This prevents teams from developing problem-solving capability
- Creates single point of failure (Agnes)
- Doesn't build organizational resilience

**Key Insight:** Starfleet was "running up against a wall" so leadership moved Agnes there. This might help short-term but creates long-term problems: teams don't learn, Agnes becomes bottleneck, organization doesn't develop depth. Better: Agnes coaches teams to solve problems themselves, or systemic issues get addressed so teams don't hit walls.

---

### 8. **New Managers Need to Investigate, Not Assume**

**Core Learning:** When taking over a struggling situation, effective managers gather data from multiple perspectives before acting.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Laris is new and needs to build understanding
- Different stakeholders have different perspectives
- Must understand both stated and unstated issues
- Investigation should inform action, not vice versa

**Key Insight:** The case asks "Where should Laris dig?" This is the right question. Laris should: talk to Agnes, Elnor, Jack separately to understand their perspectives; understand the hiring process and disagreements; investigate the technical strategy alignment; understand the history and dynamics. Only after diagnosis should Laris prescribe solutions.

## Facilitation Overview

### Session Structure (Recommended: 60-75 minutes)

1. **Introduction** (5 min)
   - Frame the case and learning objectives
   - Set discussion norms

2. **Mapping the Situation** (10 min)
   - Who are the players?
   - What's happened so far?
   - What's puzzling?

3. **Stakeholder Perspectives** (15 min)
   - Agnes, Elnor, Jack, Laris, Leadership
   - How might each be contributing?

4. **Root Cause Hypotheses** (15 min)
   - People, technical, process issues
   - What might really be going on?

5. **Decision Analysis and Patterns** (10 min)
   - Examining key decisions
   - The roving engineer pattern

6. **Laris's Path Forward** (10 min)
   - Where should Laris dig?
   - What should Laris do?

7. **Synthesis and Takeaways** (10 min)
   - Key learnings
   - Application to students' contexts

---

## Facilitation Guide

### Opening the Discussion

**Goal:** Orient to the complexity and multiple stakeholders

**Suggested Opening:**
> "Laris is new to this team and encounters a puzzling situation: a critical project, a hiring process that's stuck, senior engineers who can't agree, and complex history. There's a lot going on here. Let's start by mapping what we know."

**Initial Mapping Exercise:**
"Let's list the key players and their relationships." Build this on board:
- Agnes: Roving engineer, worked with Elnor (payments scaling) and Jack (econometrics)
- Elnor: Founding Starfleet engineer
- Jack: Founding Starfleet engineer, referred Laris
- Laris: New manager, referred by Jack
- Leadership: Moves Agnes to crises, eager to ship Starfleet
- Less senior engineers: On Starfleet team

---

### Discussion Flow

#### Phase 1: Understanding the Puzzle (10 min)

**Prompt:** "Let's identify what's puzzling here. What stands out to you?"

**Capture Student Observations:**
- 8 weeks, 5 candidates, no hire on critical project
- Agnes screens "very rigorously"
- No "unanimous approval"
- Leadership "eagerly" supports Agnes
- Agnes proposed modular approach quickly after arriving
- Elnor and Jack are on hiring committee

**Key Question:**
> "What do we not know that we'd want to know to understand this situation?"

**Important Missing Information:**
- Did Elnor and Jack agree with the modular approach?
- What specific feedback is given on candidates?
- Where do evaluators disagree?
- What does "very rigorous" screening mean?
- What's the relationship quality between Agnes, Elnor, and Jack?

---

#### Phase 2: Stakeholder Perspectives (15 min)

**Prompt:** "The case explicitly asks us to consider how each stakeholder might be contributing to the problem and to the solution. Let's go through each."

**Facilitate Deep Dive on Each:**

**Agnes:**

*Contributing to problem:*
- Possibly setting unrealistic hiring bar
- May have proposed approach without full team buy-in
- Pattern of arriving, changing things, needing resources
- Might be looking for someone exactly like herself

*Contributing to solution:*
- Deep expertise and track record
- Can articulate what's needed
- Has authority from leadership
- Could adjust approach or bar

**Elnor:**

*Contributing to problem:*
- Might disagree with modular approach
- Could be protecting original Starfleet vision
- May resent Agnes's arrival and authority
- Using hiring as passive veto

*Contributing to solution:*
- Founding context and deep knowledge
- Could help calibrate hiring bar
- Could voice concerns directly
- Could support if aligned on approach

**Jack:**

*Contributing to problem:*
- Might disagree with Agnes
- Referred Laris but unclear expectations
- Could be caught between Agnes and Elnor
- May have own vision for Starfleet

*Contributing to solution:*
- Relationship with Laris (referred her)
- Might be bridge between parties
- Historical context
- Could help align team

**Laris:**

*Contributing to problem:*
- New, may not have full context
- Referred by Jack, possible perceived bias
- Hasn't yet investigated deeply
- Might be avoiding conflict

*Contributing to solution:*
- Fresh perspective
- Can investigate without history
- Authority as manager
- Can create alignment process

**Leadership:**

*Contributing to problem:*
- Pattern of using Agnes as fix-all
- May give Agnes too much authority too quickly
- Possibly don't trust existing teams
- "Desperate" creates pressure, not clarity

*Contributing to solution:*
- Can clarify authority and priorities
- Can support alignment process
- Can provide resources
- Can adjust expectations or timeline

**Discussion:**
> "Notice how many of these are about misalignment, authority, and trust—not about candidate quality. What does that tell us?"

---

#### Phase 3: Root Cause Hypotheses (15 min)

**Prompt:** "The case asks us to consider people, technical, and process issues. Let's build hypotheses in each category."

**Create Three Columns:**

**People Issues:**
- Disagreement about technical approach (Agnes vs. Elnor/Jack)
- Trust deficit from roving engineer pattern
- Resentment about Agnes getting resources
- Passive resistance through hiring
- Power dynamics unclear

**Technical Issues:**
- Modular approach not well-defined
- Disagreement about architecture
- Role requirements unclear
- Different technical philosophies
- Uncertainty about what's needed

**Process Issues:**
- Unanimous approval gives everyone veto
- No clear hiring manager/decision-maker
- Hiring bar not calibrated
- Too rigorous screening
- Feedback not actionable

**Critical Question:**
> "If you had to guess at the primary issue, what would it be? What makes you think that?"

**Guide Discussion Toward:**
Most likely this is a combination: disagreement about technical approach (technical issue) that's unexpressed (people issue) and manifesting as hiring gridlock (process issue). The unanimous approval requirement (process) enables passive resistance (people) to avoid direct conflict about strategy (technical).

---

#### Phase 4: Decision Analysis (10 min)

**Prompt:** "The case asks us to tease apart each decision and understand tradeoffs. Let's look at key decisions."

**Decision 1: Leadership Moving Agnes to Starfleet**

**Tradeoffs:**
- Pro: Brings expertise when project struggling
- Con: May undermine existing team
- Pro: Agnes has track record
- Con: Doesn't build team's capability

**Decision 2: Agnes Proposing Modular Approach**

**Tradeoffs:**
- Pro: Fresh perspective may see better approach
- Con: Only 2 weeks of context
- Pro: Modular design might be right
- Con: May not have team buy-in

**Decision 3: Creating New Sub-team and Budget**

**Tradeoffs:**
- Pro: Resources to execute new approach
- Con: Splitting team might fragment effort
- Pro: Shows leadership support
- Con: Quick agreement might bypass healthy debate

**Decision 4: Unanimous Approval for Hiring**

**Tradeoffs:**
- Pro: Ensures everyone comfortable with hire
- Con: Gives everyone veto power
- Pro: Collaborative decision-making
- Con: Can paralyze when misaligned

**Key Learning:**
> "Notice how early decisions (moving Agnes, eagerly approving her requests) might have created conditions for later problems (hiring gridlock). How could these decisions have been made differently?"

---

#### Phase 5: The Roving Engineer Pattern (10 min)

**Prompt:** "Agnes is described as a 'roving lead engineer' who gets moved to teams facing difficult challenges. Let's examine this pattern."

**Benefits:**
- Expertise brought to struggling teams
- Cross-pollination of ideas
- Organizational knowledge spreads
- Crisis resolution

**Costs/Risks:**
- Teams don't develop own problem-solving capability
- Dependence on single person
- Can create resentment
- Parachuting in might miss context
- Pattern might indicate systemic issues not being addressed

**Discussion Questions:**
- "When is this pattern helpful? When is it harmful?"
- "What might this pattern tell us about Acme's organizational health?"
- "How might Elnor and Jack feel about Agnes arriving to 'fix' their project?"

**Key Insight:**
> "The roving engineer pattern might work for acute crises, but Starfleet isn't a crisis—it's a strategic initiative that was 'just recently' started. Using the crisis-fix pattern here might be wrong approach and create resistance."

---

#### Phase 6: Where Should Laris Dig? (10-15 min)

**Prompt:** "The case asks: How can Laris figure out what might be going on and help Starfleet make progress? Where should she dig? Let's build her investigation plan."

**Build This Investigation Plan:**

**Week 1: Individual Conversations**
- **Agnes**: What are you looking for in lead engineer? What feedback on candidates? What's the modular approach and why? How's working with Elnor and Jack?
- **Elnor**: What's your perspective on Starfleet progress? On modular approach? On candidates? What do you need?
- **Jack**: Same questions. Plus: Why did you refer Laris? What did you hope she'd do?
- **Less senior engineers**: How's the project going? What's working? What's not?

**What to Listen For:**
- Alignment or misalignment on technical approach
- Who agrees/disagrees with what
- Stated vs. unstated concerns
- Relationship quality and trust

**Week 1-2: Process Investigation**
- Review hiring feedback on all 5 candidates
- Understand where evaluators disagreed
- Examine hiring bar and role definition
- Assess whether unanimous approval is right process

**Week 2: Facilitated Alignment**
- Bring Agnes, Elnor, Jack together
- Surface technical disagreements
- Discuss modular approach explicitly
- Align on role needs and hiring bar
- Agree on decision-making process

**Ongoing: Leadership Communication**
- Update leadership on what you're finding
- Set realistic expectations
- Ask for support in alignment process
- Clarify authority if needed

**Critical Questions to Surface in Investigation:**
1. Is everyone aligned on the technical approach?
2. Is the role well-defined and realistic?
3. Is the hiring bar calibrated to market and urgency?
4. Is the decision-making process working?
5. Are there trust or relationship issues to address?

---

### Handling Difficult Moments

#### If Students Focus Only on Hiring Process

**Intervention:**
> "We're talking a lot about the hiring process. But step back: Why would three smart, experienced engineers struggle to agree on five candidates? What might that signal about something deeper?"

*Guide toward seeing hiring as symptom of strategy/alignment issues.*

#### If Students Blame Agnes

**Intervention:**
> "Agnes has a track record of solving hard problems. Leadership trusts her. But we're seeing resistance. Is Agnes the problem, or is something about how she's being used or how the team works creating issues?"

*Redirect to systemic view.*

#### If Students Miss the Roving Engineer Pattern

**Intervention:**
> "The case gives us Agnes's history: protocol group, payments team with Elnor, econometrics with Jack, now Starfleet. She's been moved many times. Why might the case tell us this? What might it mean?"

*Surface the pattern and its implications.*

#### If Discussion Stays Abstract

**Intervention:**
> "Let's get concrete. You're Laris. Monday morning. First thing you do is...?"

## Key Teaching Points to Ensure Coverage

Make sure these concepts emerge during discussion:

### ✓ **Hiring gridlock signals deeper problems**
- Usually about strategy, not candidates
- Process conflicts mask real disagreements
- Must address underlying issue

### ✓ **Hiring bars must be calibrated**
- Perfect candidates may not exist
- Must balance quality with reality
- Compare to market, not to yourself

### ✓ **Unanimous approval can paralyze**
- Gives everyone veto power
- Works only with high alignment
- Need clear decision-makers

### ✓ **Roving expert pattern has costs**
- Short-term help, long-term dependency
- Can create resentment
- Prevents organizational learning

### ✓ **Technical disagreements disguise as process issues**
- Surface the real debate
- Align on strategy before hiring
- Make conflicts explicit

### ✓ **Authority must be clear**
- Who decides what?
- Ambiguity creates gridlock
- Explicit frameworks help

### ✓ **New managers should investigate deeply**
- Multiple perspectives needed
- Understand before acting
- Surface unstated conflicts

**Personal Application:**

**Key Takeaways:**
> "What's the most important thing you learned about organizational dynamics from this case?"

**Commitment to Action:**
> "What will you do differently when you encounter hiring disagreements or organizational conflicts?"

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

### ❌ **Treating This as Only a Hiring Problem**
It's a strategy and alignment problem manifesting in hiring.

### ❌ **Missing the Roving Engineer Pattern**
This is central to understanding the dynamics.

### ❌ **Blaming Individuals vs. Seeing System**
The problem is systemic, not just individual behavior.

### ❌ **Not Examining Each Stakeholder's Perspective**
All five contribute; must understand each.

### ❌ **Accepting Unanimous Approval as Reasonable**
It's contributing to the problem.

### ❌ **Skipping the Decision Analysis**
Understanding past decisions explains current state.

## Assessment: What Good Discussion Looks Like

Students are learning well if they:

- ✅ Recognize hiring gridlock as symptom of deeper issues
- ✅ Consider all five stakeholder perspectives
- ✅ Examine people, technical, and process dimensions
- ✅ Analyze the roving engineer pattern critically
- ✅ See how past decisions created current situation
- ✅ Identify investigation plan for Laris
- ✅ Distinguish between individual blame and systemic view
- ✅ Connect to their own experiences with hiring and alignment

## Follow-Up Activities (Optional)

### Writing Exercise
Ask students to write:
1. Laris's investigation plan (300 words)
2. The conversation Laris should have with Agnes, Elnor, and Jack together (400 words)
3. A policy for using roving engineers effectively (200 words)

### Small Group Work
Divide into groups:
- Group A: You are Agnes. Prepare your perspective for conversation with Laris.
- Group B: You are Elnor and Jack. Prepare your perspective.
- Group C: You are Laris. Prepare your facilitation plan.

Then role-play the conversation.

### Case Study Creation
Ask students to write about a time when hiring got stuck. What was really going on? How was it resolved (or not)?
