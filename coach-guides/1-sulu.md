# Sulu Case Study: Coach Guide

## Key Management Learnings

This section outlines the critical management concepts and skills that students should develop through discussion of this case study.

### 1. **Urgency Creates False Dichotomies**

**Core Learning:** When under pressure, managers often frame problems as binary choices when multiple creative options exist.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- "Hire quickly" vs. "keep searching" is a false dichotomy
- Urgency narrows thinking—good managers actively resist this
- The best solutions often come from questioning the constraints themselves
- Reframing the problem can reveal hidden options

**Key Insight:** Sulu is trapped in either/or thinking. Better questions include: Can we negotiate the timeline? Can we break the work into phases? Can we bring in contractors or consultants? Can existing team members take on pieces? Can we partner with external experts?

---

### 2. **Hiring Delays Have Compounding Costs**

**Core Learning:** Every week spent without the right person compounds the problem. Managers must treat hiring with appropriate urgency.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Three months passed without a hire—this represents 50% of available time lost
- Hiring isn't "in addition to" the work; it IS the work
- Delayed hiring decisions get progressively harder, not easier
- Passive recruiting rarely works for specialized roles

**Key Insight:** Sulu should have been proactive from day one: engaging recruiters, leveraging networks, potentially offering incentives, or even considering acqui-hires. The first three months should have included parallel strategies, not just posting and hoping.

---

### 3. **Timeline Commitments Require Risk Assessment**

**Core Learning:** When making commitments, managers must explicitly analyze risks and build in buffers, not assume best-case scenarios.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Sulu's timeline assumes everything goes perfectly (3 months to hire specialist, 3 months to execute)
- No buffer for hiring delays, technical complexity, or learning curves
- Risk management means planning for likely problems, not hoping they won't occur
- Commitments should be based on realistic worst-case or expected-case, not best-case

**Key Insight:** A 9-month deadline with 3-month specialist search + 3-month execution leaves zero margin for error. This was predictably risky. Sulu should have either negotiated a longer timeline, started hiring immediately before the opportunity was confirmed, or had contingency plans.

---

### 4. **Communication Failures Amplify Problems**

**Core Learning:** Not communicating risks early and often to stakeholders turns difficult situations into crises.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Leadership likely doesn't know Sulu has been unable to hire for 3 months
- Surprises erode trust faster than almost anything else
- Early escalation creates options; late escalation forces bad choices
- "I'm working on it" is not the same as "Here's the risk and here's what I need"

**Key Insight:** Sulu should have been providing regular updates: "Week 4: No qualified candidates yet. If we don't have someone by Week 8, I recommend we consider X, Y, Z." This gives leadership time to help—potentially opening budget for higher compensation, approving contractors, or negotiating timeline with government partners.

---

### 5. **Specialization Creates Organizational Vulnerability**

**Core Learning:** Teams with unique, non-transferable knowledge create single points of failure. This requires intentional mitigation.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- The Carboni team's highly specialized nature is both strength and weakness
- Over-specialization means small teams can't absorb unexpected work
- Knowledge sharing, documentation, and cross-training reduce this risk
- Sometimes "good enough" generalists are strategically better than perfect specialists

**Key Insight:** If only one person can do this work, the organization is fragile. Sulu should be thinking: How do we build depth? How do we document tribal knowledge? How do we make our expertise more transferable?

---

### 6. **Perfect Can Be the Enemy of Good (Hiring Edition)**

**Core Learning:** Holding out for the "perfect" candidate often means missing out on great candidates who could succeed with support.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- The "perfect" candidate may not exist, may not be looking, or may not choose Acme
- A strong generalist with coaching can often outperform a specialist without support
- Cultural fit, learning ability, and motivation often matter more than existing domain knowledge
- Hiring is a bet on potential, not just current skills

**Key Insight:** The question isn't "specialist vs. generalist" but "who can be successful in this role with the support we can provide?" A motivated learner with strong fundamentals might be better than a domain expert who doesn't fit the team culture.

---

### 7. **Scope and Requirements Are Negotiable Until They're Not**

**Core Learning:** Technical requirements often have more flexibility than initially assumed. Managers should challenge scope before accepting it as fixed.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- "This requires 6 months of work" is an estimate, not a law of physics
- Scope can often be reduced, phased, or simplified
- MVP thinking applies to government contracts too
- Asking "What's the minimum needed for launch?" opens conversations

**Key Insight:** Has Sulu questioned what "complete the changes needed for this initiative" actually means? Could Phase 1 be delivered in 6 months with Phase 2 coming later? Could some features be deprioritized? Exploring this might reveal flexibility.

---

### 8. **Other Projects and Team Capacity Are Connected**

**Core Learning:** Teams don't operate in isolation. Hiring for one project affects all projects. Leaders must consider the full portfolio.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- The Carboni team has "hard commitments" and revenue targets for IPO
- Adding one new person (who needs training) actually reduces team capacity short-term
- Training takes time from existing team members
- Portfolio management means making trade-offs across all work

**Key Insight:** Even if Sulu hires someone tomorrow, the existing team must train them while also delivering on IPO commitments. This might not be feasible. Sulu needs to think about total team capacity, not just this one project.

---

## Facilitation Overview

### Session Structure (Recommended: 60-75 minutes)

1. **Introduction** (5 min)
   - Frame the case and learning objectives
   - Set discussion norms

2. **Initial Perspectives** (10 min)
   - Quick poll: What should Sulu do?
   - Surface initial reasoning

3. **Timeline Reality Check** (15 min)
   - Work through the math together
   - Identify hidden assumptions

4. **Options Exploration** (15-20 min)
   - Beyond the binary choice
   - Creative problem-solving

5. **Root Cause and Prevention** (10-15 min)
   - What should Sulu have done earlier?
   - Communication and risk management

6. **Synthesis and Takeaways** (10 min)
   - Key learnings
   - Application to students' contexts

---

## Facilitation Guide

### Opening the Discussion

**Goal:** Surface the time pressure and binary framing

**Suggested Opening:**
> "Sulu is facing a common management challenge: a critical deadline, a specialized skill gap, and a hiring market that isn't cooperating. Let's start by understanding the situation fully before jumping to solutions."

**Initial Poll (Anonymous or Show of Hands):**
- Hire the next strong engineer and train them
- Keep searching for a specialist
- Something else entirely

*Note the distribution. If most choose one of the first two, note that we'll explore whether these are the only options.*

---

### Discussion Flow

#### Phase 1: Understanding the Timeline (10-15 min)

**Prompt:** "Let's do the math together. Sulu has 6 months. Walk me through what has to happen in those 6 months."

**Facilitate Building This on Whiteboard/Virtual Board:**

**Option 1: Hire Generalist**
- Find and close candidate: __ weeks
- Onboarding: __ weeks
- Domain training: 3 months (12 weeks)
- Actual work: __ weeks
- **Total: __ weeks. Does this fit in 24 weeks?**

**Option 2: Find Specialist**
- Find and close specialist: __ weeks
- Onboarding: __ weeks
- Actual work: 3 months (12 weeks)
- **Total: __ weeks. Does this fit in 24 weeks?**

**Guided Questions:**
- "What's a realistic time to close a strong engineering candidate?"
- "The case says 3 months for execution. Does that include any buffer?"
- "What if something goes wrong? Where's the slack?"

**Key Point to Surface:**
Both options are extremely tight. Option 1 has almost no execution time. Option 2 assumes finding someone quickly, which contradicts the last 3 months of experience.

**Critical Observation:**
> "Notice what we've discovered: neither of the obvious options looks great when we do the math. What does that tell us?"

**Answer to Draw Out:** When both obvious paths seem unlikely to work, we need to either change the paths or change the constraints.

---

#### Phase 2: Questioning Assumptions (15-20 min)

**Prompt:** "Let's identify what Sulu might be assuming that could actually be negotiable or flexible."

**Capture Student Ideas:**

**Assumptions About Scope:**
- Does ALL the work need to be done for launch?
- Could this be phased?
- What's the MVP?

**Assumptions About Resources:**
- Does it have to be one person?
- Could consultants/contractors help?
- Could another team contribute?

**Assumptions About Timeline:**
- Is 9 months (now 6) actually fixed?
- Who set this deadline and why?
- What's the cost of a 2-month delay vs. failure?

**Assumptions About Hiring:**
- Does the person need to be full-time hire?
- Could Sulu acqui-hire a small company?
- Could Sulu offer equity/compensation to attract faster?

**Guided Questions:**
- "What would Sulu need to know to challenge these assumptions?"
- "Who could help Sulu get creative here?"
- "What's scary about questioning the constraints?"

**Alternative Approaches Students Might Suggest:**

1. **Negotiate Timeline**
   - Talk to stakeholders about 6-month reality
   - Propose phased delivery
   - Get help from leadership in government discussions

2. **Reduce Scope**
   - Identify true MVP for government initiative
   - Phase 1: Minimum viable, Phase 2: Full features
   - Buy time for better hiring

3. **Alternative Resourcing**
   - Bring in contractors for 6-month engagement
   - Hire consulting firm with domain expertise
   - Borrow engineer from another company (unusual but possible)

4. **Hybrid Approach**
   - Hire best available person NOW
   - Also bring in contractor/consultant
   - Consultant delivers, new hire learns

5. **Team Reorganization**
   - Could existing team members shift priorities?
   - What if another project is deprioritized?
   - Is this really the highest-value work?

**For Each Alternative, Discuss:**
- "What would need to be true for this to work?"
- "What are the costs and risks?"
- "How would Sulu pitch this to leadership?"

---

#### Phase 3: The First Three Months (10-15 min)

**Prompt:** "The case asks: Is there something Sulu should have been doing during those first three months? Let's build that list."

**What Sulu Could Have Been Doing:**

**Week 1-2:**
- [ ] Engage specialized recruiting firms
- [ ] Map the candidate market (how many people globally have these skills?)
- [ ] Identify compensation needed to attract specialists
- [ ] Start networking in domain-specific communities

**Week 3-4:**
- [ ] If no strong candidates, escalate to leadership
- [ ] Explore contractor/consultant options in parallel
- [ ] Question scope with stakeholders
- [ ] Assess if existing team can contribute

**Week 6-8:**
- [ ] If still no candidates, pivot strategy
- [ ] Potentially explore acqui-hire
- [ ] Communicate risks clearly to leadership
- [ ] Develop contingency plans

**Week 10-12:**
- [ ] Should have made a decision by now
- [ ] Either hired someone or escalated for help
- [ ] Should NOT still be passively searching

**Key Learning:**
"What pattern do you notice? Sulu treated hiring as a passive activity—post and wait. What would active hiring look like?"

**Active Hiring Includes:**
- Proactive sourcing and outreach
- Parallel strategies (hire + contract + consult)
- Regular communication with stakeholders
- Willingness to pivot quickly when approach isn't working

---

#### Phase 4: Communication to Leadership (10 min)

**Prompt:** "One of the discussion prompts asks: What should Sulu communicate up the management chain? Let's practice this."

**Exercise: Draft the Email/Message**

Break into small groups or do together:
> "You're Sulu. It's been 3 months. Write the message you should send to your manager and relevant stakeholders."

**What Should Be Included:**
- Current situation (no hire yet after 3 months)
- Timeline implications (only 6 months left)
- Risks with each option
- What you need from leadership (help negotiating timeline, budget for consultants, etc.)
- Your recommendation

**Good Example:**
> "We're 3 months into hiring for the government initiative role with no successful hire. With 6 months to deadline, I need to share the timeline risk and get your input on options..."

**Bad Example:**
> "Still searching for the right candidate. Will keep you posted."

**Discussion:**
- "Why is the first better than the second?"
- "What does early, transparent communication enable?"
- "How might leadership be able to help if they know soon enough?"

**Key Insight:** Leaders can't help with problems they don't know about. Risk communication isn't admitting failure; it's enabling better decision-making.

---

#### Phase 5: Other Projects and Team Capacity (10 min)

**Prompt:** "The case mentions the team has 'hard commitments including features needed to hit revenue targets for an upcoming IPO.' How does this affect our thinking?"

**Guided Questions:**
- "If Sulu hires a generalist who needs 3 months of training, who trains them?"
- "What's the opportunity cost on other projects?"
- "Could this new work actually jeopardize the IPO commitments?"

**Facilitate Discussion On:**
- Total team capacity vs. total demand
- Training costs on existing team
- Portfolio prioritization

**Critical Question:**
> "What if the right answer is actually: 'We can't do this project without jeopardizing other commitments'? How would you, as Sulu, make that case?"

**Key Insight:** Sometimes the best management decision is saying "we can't do both well" and forcing a prioritization conversation rather than promising to do everything and failing at all of it.

---

### Handling Difficult Moments

#### If Students Fixate on Binary Choice

**Intervention:**
> "I notice we keep coming back to 'hire generalist or keep searching.' But we calculated that neither has good odds of success. What if both options are bad? What does that mean we should do?"

#### If Students Blame Sulu Harshly

**Intervention:**
> "It's easy to see Sulu's mistakes in hindsight. But think about the pressures: exciting new opportunity, optimism about hiring market, lots of other work. How does a manager avoid falling into this trap?"

*Redirect to systems and processes, not personal fault.*

#### If Discussion Stays Too Abstract

**Intervention:**
> "Let's get concrete. You're Sulu. It's Monday morning. What are the first three actions you take?"

#### If Students Avoid the Hard Trade-offs

**Intervention:**
> "I'm hearing a lot of 'Sulu should do X AND Y AND Z.' But Sulu has limited time and limited team capacity. If you had to choose ONE thing to do first, what would it be and why?"

## Key Teaching Points to Ensure Coverage

Make sure these concepts emerge during discussion:

### ✓ **Time is a constraint that compounds**
- Three lost months can't be recovered
- Hiring must be treated with appropriate urgency
- Passive approaches rarely work for specialized roles

### ✓ **Constraints are often more negotiable than they appear**
- Scope, timeline, and resources can all be questioned
- Creative problem-solving comes from challenging assumptions
- Best solutions often involve changing the frame, not just making the choice

### ✓ **Communication creates options**
- Early risk escalation enables help
- Leaders can't solve problems they don't know about
- Transparency builds trust even when news is bad

### ✓ **Perfect is the enemy of good**
- The "perfect" candidate may not exist or may not be available
- A good candidate with support often beats a great candidate you can't find
- Hiring is about potential and fit, not just current skills

### ✓ **Portfolio thinking matters**
- Projects don't exist in isolation
- Team capacity is finite
- Sometimes the right answer is "we can't do everything"

### ✓ **Risk management requires buffers**
- Best-case planning is not risk management
- Commitments should assume things go somewhat wrong
- Zero buffer means certain failure when anything goes wrong

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

### ❌ **Accepting the Binary Frame**
Push students beyond "A or B" to "what else is possible?"

### ❌ **Ignoring the Timeline Math**
Make students do the actual calculation—it reveals the problem clearly.

### ❌ **Forgetting About Other Work**
The team has other commitments. This affects everything.

### ❌ **Blaming Sulu Without Learning**
"Sulu messed up" is not a useful insight. "Here's what prevents this" is.

### ❌ **Assuming Hiring Is Someone Else's Job**
For managers, hiring IS the job, especially for critical roles.

### ❌ **Treating Constraints as Fixed**
Most constraints can be negotiated or reconsidered.

## Assessment: What Good Discussion Looks Like

Students are learning well if they:

- ✅ Challenge the binary framing and explore creative alternatives
- ✅ Do realistic timeline math rather than wishful thinking
- ✅ Recognize the importance of early communication
- ✅ Consider total team capacity, not just the new project
- ✅ Distinguish between assumptions and facts
- ✅ Discuss both what Sulu should do now and what could have been different
- ✅ Apply learnings to their own situations
- ✅ Show empathy while also holding Sulu accountable

## Follow-Up Activities (Optional)

### Writing Exercise
Ask students to write:
1. The message Sulu should send to leadership today (200 words)
2. A hiring playbook for future specialized roles (300 words)
3. A risk assessment Sulu should have done at week 0 (200 words)

### Small Group Work
Divide into groups:
- Group A: You are Sulu's manager. What advice do you give?
- Group B: You are a hiring consultant. What strategy do you recommend?
- Group C: You are the government stakeholder. How flexible can you be on timeline?

### Role-Play Exercise
Set up a conversation:
- Person 1: Sulu
- Person 2: Sulu's manager
- Scenario: Week 12, no hire yet. Sulu needs to communicate the situation.
