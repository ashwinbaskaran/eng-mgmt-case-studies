# Worf Case Study: Coach Guide

## Key Management Learnings

This section outlines the critical management concepts and skills that students should develop through discussion of this case study.

### 1. **Career Development Is an Ongoing Responsibility, Not a Response to Requests**

**Core Learning:** Great managers proactively create development opportunities and have ongoing career conversations, rather than reactively responding when someone asks for a promotion.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Worf is in reactive mode—responding to Troi's request rather than having shaped her path proactively
- Career conversations should happen regularly (quarterly or monthly), not just during reviews or when someone asks
- Managers should know what each team member wants and be actively creating pathways
- Waiting for someone to ask means you've waited too long

**Key Insight:** The case asks "What could Worf have done earlier?" The answer: regular career conversations where Troi's interests and Worf's observations about her strengths could have led to intentional planning, not improvisation.

---

### 2. **Understanding Motivation Matters More Than Granting Requests**

**Core Learning:** When someone asks for a specific role or title, the manager's job is to understand the underlying motivation, not just fulfill the literal request.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Troi says she wants to "be an engineering manager"
- But what does she really want? Impact? Autonomy? Mentorship? Recognition? Growth?
- Different motivations suggest different solutions
- The conversation should explore "why" before "how"

**Key Insight:** Effective managers ask: "What about the management role appeals to you?" "What did you enjoy most during your time as acting manager?" "What are you hoping to do or achieve?" The answers reveal whether the solution is a management title, or something else entirely.

---

### 3. **Constraints Often Breed Creativity**

**Core Learning:** Limited resources force creative solutions that can be better than the obvious approach with unlimited resources.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- "How many managers does a team of six need?" seems to limit options
- But constraints force innovation: hybrid roles, new team structures, cross-team opportunities
- Sometimes the best solutions emerge from constraints
- Scarcity of traditional options reveals non-traditional possibilities

**Key Insight:** Worf can't just "make Troi a manager of 3 people" without considering efficiency and impact. This constraint might lead to better solutions: tech lead role with management aspects, managing a new initiative, cross-team leadership, or growing into a role as the team expands.

---

### 4. **Team Composition Changes What's Possible**

**Core Learning:** The nature of the team—seniority, independence, work style—affects what structures and solutions will work.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Acme Apps is "senior, broadly knowledgeable, independent, intrinsically motivated"
- This team might not need intensive management
- Could potentially operate with unconventional structures
- Tech lead or player-coach models might work here where they wouldn't elsewhere

**Key Insight:** A team of junior engineers needs more hands-on management. A team of senior, independent contributors might thrive with minimal management and strong tech leadership. The solution should fit the team's actual needs, not just organizational convention.

---

### 5. **Growth Planning Has Multiple Dimensions**

**Core Learning:** When evaluating options, consider: team growth, company growth, role evolution, and individual development timelines.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- If Acme Apps team will grow (12 people), splitting makes sense
- If company is growing, other management opportunities may emerge
- If neither is growing, creative solutions become essential
- Timing matters—what's available now vs. in 6 months

**Key Insight:** The case explicitly asks about growth scenarios. This teaches students to think probabilistically: "If X happens, then Y becomes possible. How likely is X? How can I influence X?" Good managers actively shape growth opportunities, not just wait for them.

---

### 6. **Internal Mobility Benefits Organizations and Individuals**

**Core Learning:** Companies that enable internal career development retain talent, build institutional knowledge, and maintain culture.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Acme "values and encourages internal mobility and growth"
- This is a strategic advantage—people stay longer, learn more, contribute more
- Troi wants to stay at Acme; this loyalty is valuable
- Supporting internal moves is often better than external hiring

**Key Insight:** Worf should leverage Acme's culture. He can ask: "What other teams might need managers soon?" "Can we create a new role?" "How can leadership help?" An organization that values mobility should provide mechanisms to enable it.

---

### 7. **Acting Roles Create Expectations That Must Be Managed**

**Core Learning:** When someone takes an interim or acting role, there should be clarity about what it means for their future.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Troi "thrived" as acting manager—she proved herself
- Acting roles are auditions; success creates expectations
- Without explicit conversation, people assume success = promotion
- Managers should clarify upfront what acting roles mean

**Key Insight:** Before his sabbatical, Worf could have said: "This is a development opportunity. If it goes well, we'll talk about what's next for you. There may not be an immediate management opening, but we'll work together to find the right path." This frames expectations and shows commitment.

---

### 8. **The Empathy-Decision Balance**

**Core Learning:** Managers must balance empathy and support for individuals with making sound organizational decisions.

**What Students Should Understand:**
- Troi "earned the right to be supported"—her contribution deserves recognition
- But creating an inefficient organizational structure to grant her wish helps no one long-term
- True support means finding the right opportunity, not just saying yes
- Empathy includes honest conversations about constraints and alternatives

**Key Insight:** The case asks whether Worf needs "empathy, active listening, negotiation, or decision-making skills." The answer: all of them. Great managers hold the tension between supporting individuals and serving organizational needs.

## Facilitation Overview

### Session Structure (Recommended: 60-75 minutes)

1. **Introduction** (5 min)
   - Frame the case and learning objectives
   - Set discussion norms

2. **Understanding Motivations** (10-15 min)
   - What does Troi really want?
   - What did she experience as acting manager?

3. **Exploring Options** (20 min)
   - Beyond the binary choice
   - Team composition and creative solutions

4. **Growth Scenarios** (10-15 min)
   - How do different futures change the options?
   - What can Worf influence?

5. **Root Cause and Prevention** (10 min)
   - What could Worf have done earlier?
   - Career development best practices

6. **Synthesis and Takeaways** (10 min)
   - Key learnings
   - Application to students' contexts

---

## Facilitation Guide

### Opening the Discussion

**Goal:** Surface the constraint and the competing obligations

**Suggested Opening:**
> "Worf faces a wonderful problem: a talented engineer who proved herself and wants to grow into management. But there's a constraint—his team is only 6 people. How do we honor Troi's development while maintaining organizational effectiveness?"

**Initial Poll (Anonymous or Show of Hands):**
- Split the team so Troi can manage 3 people
- Find Troi another team to manage
- Create a different kind of role for Troi
- Something else

*Note the distribution and variety of approaches students are considering.*

---

### Discussion Flow

#### Phase 1: Understanding Troi's Motivation (10-15 min)

**Prompt:** "Before we solve for Worf, let's understand Troi. What does she actually want? The case says she 'would like to be an engineering manager'—but what does that mean to her?"

**Guided Questions:**
- "What did Troi likely do during those 3 months?"
- "What did she enjoy about the acting manager role?"
- "What might she want that the management role represents?"

**Facilitate Building This List:**

**Possible Motivations:**
- **Impact**: Ability to shape team direction and strategy
- **Mentorship**: Coaching and developing others
- **Influence**: Voice in organizational decisions
- **Growth**: Learning new skills
- **Recognition**: Title and status
- **Compensation**: Pay associated with management
- **Autonomy**: Freedom to make decisions
- **Relationships**: Closer connection with team members

**Critical Question:**
> "Here's what's interesting: different motivations suggest different solutions. If Troi wants mentorship opportunities, she might not need to be THE manager. If she wants organizational influence, there might be other paths. Why does this matter?"

**Answer to Draw Out:** You can't solve the problem until you understand what problem you're solving. Worf needs to have deep conversations with Troi about what she actually wants before designing solutions.

**Role-Play Exercise (Optional but Powerful):**
> "Let's practice the conversation Worf should have with Troi. I need two volunteers..."

**Worf should ask questions like:**
- "What did you enjoy most about managing while I was away?"
- "What was challenging?"
- "What are you hoping a management role would give you?"
- "What does success look like for you in 2-3 years?"

---

#### Phase 2: Team Composition and Structure (10-15 min)

**Prompt:** "The case specifically describes the team as 'senior, broadly knowledgeable, independent, intrinsically motivated.' Why does this matter? How does this affect what's possible?"

**Guided Questions:**
- "How much management does this team actually need?"
- "Would splitting into two 3-person teams make sense for this type of team?"
- "What structures might work better for senior, independent engineers?"

**Facilitate Discussion On:**

**Traditional Management Model:**
- Manager handles people development, conflict, process
- Manager shields team from organizational friction
- Manager sets priorities and direction
- Works well for: mixed-seniority teams, complex people dynamics

**Tech Lead / Player-Coach Model:**
- Technical leadership with some management responsibility
- Less hierarchy, more collaboration
- Still provides growth and recognition
- Works well for: senior, independent teams

**Key Question:**
> "What does this particular team need from leadership? Is it the same as what a team of new grads needs?"

**Answer to Surface:** Different teams need different things. Blindly applying org-chart conventions misses opportunities to design roles that fit actual needs.

---

#### Phase 3: Creative Options Exploration (15-20 min)

**Prompt:** "Let's brainstorm options beyond the obvious. What creative solutions might exist here?"

**Capture Student Ideas:**

**Option Category 1: Restructure Current Team**
1. Split into two 3-person teams (Worf leads one, Troi leads other)
2. Worf moves to different role, Troi takes the whole team
3. Create tech lead role for Troi with management elements
4. Troi becomes manager, Worf moves to managing managers

**Option Category 2: Create New Opportunity**
1. Troi manages a new initiative/project within Apps scope
2. Troi manages a cross-team function (platform, tools, etc.)
3. Troi starts new team for emerging need
4. Rotate: Troi manages for 6 months, Worf mentors and does IC work

**Option Category 3: Find Existing Opportunity**
1. Another team at Acme needs manager
2. Troi moves to adjacent team where manager is leaving
3. Troi takes over a struggling team that needs her strength

**Option Category 4: Hybrid/Interim Approaches**
1. Troi as "manager in training" with gradual responsibility
2. Shared management model (both manage, divided responsibilities)
3. Troi manages specific functions (hiring, onboarding) while learning
4. 6-month plan: if team grows to 10, split then; prepare in meantime

**For Each Option, Discuss:**
- "What would make this work well?"
- "What are the risks?"
- "What does this require from the organization?"
- "How does this serve Troi's development?"
- "How does this serve the team and business?"

**Key Point to Surface:**
The best solution is likely not "make Troi a manager of 3 people" but something more creative that both develops Troi AND makes organizational sense.

---

#### Phase 4: Growth Scenarios and Influence (10-15 min)

**Prompt:** "The case explicitly asks: What if the team is likely to grow? What if the company is growing? Let's think through these scenarios."

**Build This Matrix Together:**

| Scenario | Implication for Options | What Worf Can Do |
|----------|------------------------|------------------|
| Acme Apps team will grow to 12+ | Splitting makes sense; plan for it | Accelerate hiring, prepare split |
| Acme Apps team won't grow | Need different solution | Create role or find other team |
| Company is growing | Other teams may need managers | Network Troi, explore options |
| Company isn't growing | Very limited management openings | Consider hybrid/tech lead paths |

**Critical Questions:**
> "How likely is each scenario? How can Worf find out? More importantly, can Worf *influence* any of these?"

**Discussion Points:**
- Can Worf make the case for team growth?
- Can Worf identify needs that would justify new teams?
- Can Worf advocate with leadership for opportunities for Troi?
- Can Worf work with peers to find openings?

**Key Insight:** Managers aren't passive recipients of organizational constraints. Good managers actively shape opportunities by making cases for growth, identifying needs, and advocating for their people.

---

#### Phase 5: What Worf Could Have Done Earlier (10 min)

**Prompt:** "The case asks: What could Worf have done earlier that might help now? Let's build that list."

**Before the Sabbatical:**
- [ ] Discuss with Troi what the acting role means
- [ ] Clarify whether it's a development opportunity vs. audition for promotion
- [ ] Set expectations about what happens when he returns
- [ ] Make commitments about career conversations

**During Regular 1:1s (Before Sabbatical):**
- [ ] Understand Troi's career aspirations
- [ ] Discuss what she wants to learn
- [ ] Create development plan
- [ ] Look for opportunities proactively

**Planning for Sabbatical:**
- [ ] Be explicit: "This is a chance to try management. Let's see how it goes and discuss what's next when I return"
- [ ] Frame it as data-gathering, not commitment

**Upon Return:**
- [ ] Immediate conversation about her experience
- [ ] Not wait for "first meeting" weeks later
- [ ] Proactive planning conversation

**Key Learning:**
> "What pattern do you notice? These are all proactive career development practices. What would your work be like if your manager did these things with you?"

**The Proactive Career Development Model:**
1. Regular conversations (not just annual reviews)
2. Understanding motivation and aspirations
3. Creating opportunities intentionally
4. Clear communication about what things mean
5. Active advocacy and path-finding

---

### Phase 6: The Conversation Worf Should Have (10 min)

**Prompt:** "Let's get practical. What should Worf say to Troi? Let's draft the opening of that conversation."

**Small Group Exercise or Full Group:**
> "You're Worf. You're in the meeting with Troi. She's just said she enjoyed the management role and wants to be an EM. What do you say next?"

**Good Response Elements:**
- Acknowledge her success and contribution
- Express genuine commitment to her development
- Ask questions to understand motivation
- Be honest about constraints
- Commit to working together on finding the path
- Set timeline for follow-up

**Example:**
> "Troi, I'm so glad you brought this up. You did an amazing job while I was away, and it's clear you have real strength in management. I want to support your development—you've absolutely earned that. Help me understand what you enjoyed most about the role and what you're hoping for in your career. There may not be an immediate path to you managing half this team, but I'm committed to finding the right opportunity for you, whether that's here or elsewhere at Acme. Let's talk through what you want, explore options together, and make a plan."

**Bad Response Examples:**
- "We'll see what we can do." (vague, no commitment)
- "The team isn't big enough for two managers." (shuts down conversation)
- "Sure, let's split the team." (knee-jerk solution without thought)

---

### Handling Difficult Moments

#### If Students Jump to "Split the Team" Without Analysis

**Intervention:**
> "I hear 'split the team' as an option. Let's pressure-test that. What would two 3-person teams look like? Would they have enough work? Are there natural divisions in the customer base? Is this efficient?"

#### If Students Suggest Troi Should Just Leave Acme

**Intervention:**
> "Troi explicitly wants to stay at Acme. What does that tell us about what she values? And what does it tell us about what Acme should want to do?"

*Redirect to internal mobility as organizational strength.*

#### If Discussion Avoids the Hard Trade-offs

**Intervention:**
> "I'm hearing lots of 'Worf should support Troi.' Absolutely. But what if supporting her means honest conversation that the role she wants isn't immediately available? Is that okay? How do you balance empathy with honesty?"

#### If Students Focus Only on Troi, Not Team/Org

**Intervention:**
> "We've talked a lot about what Troi wants. What do the other 5 engineers on the team need? What does the organization need? How do we balance these?"

## Key Teaching Points to Ensure Coverage

Make sure these concepts emerge during discussion:

### ✓ **Career development is proactive, not reactive**
- Regular conversations, not waiting for requests
- Understanding aspirations before crises
- Creating pathways intentionally

### ✓ **Understand motivation before solving**
- What someone asks for may not be what they actually need
- Different motivations suggest different solutions
- Deep listening reveals better paths

### ✓ **Team composition affects structure**
- Senior, independent teams have different needs
- Don't apply one-size-fits-all org models
- Design roles that fit actual team needs

### ✓ **Constraints breed creativity**
- Limited options force innovative thinking
- Best solutions often come from working within constraints
- Question whether constraints are real or assumed

### ✓ **Growth scenarios change options**
- Think probabilistically about futures
- Managers can influence growth, not just react to it
- Timeline matters—what's possible in 3 vs. 6 vs. 12 months

### ✓ **Internal mobility is strategic**
- Retention of talented, culturally-aligned people
- Institutional knowledge and relationships
- Organizations should enable development

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

### ❌ **Jumping to Solutions Without Understanding Motivation**
Push students to explore what Troi actually wants first.

### ❌ **Accepting "Split the Team" Without Analysis**
Make students think through whether this actually makes sense.

### ❌ **Treating Organizational Constraints as Fixed**
Most constraints can be influenced or worked around creatively.

### ❌ **Ignoring the Other 5 Team Members**
They have needs and perspectives too.

### ❌ **Forgetting That Worf Could Have Been Proactive Earlier**
This is a key learning about preventive career development.

### ❌ **Viewing Empathy and Hard Decisions as Opposites**
Great managers do both simultaneously.

## Assessment: What Good Discussion Looks Like

Students are learning well if they:

- ✅ Explore motivation before jumping to solutions
- ✅ Consider team composition in their recommendations
- ✅ Generate creative options beyond obvious choices
- ✅ Think about growth scenarios and probability
- ✅ Recognize the importance of proactive career development
- ✅ Balance empathy for Troi with organizational needs
- ✅ Practice actual conversations, not just strategy
- ✅ Connect to their own experiences with managers

## Follow-Up Activities (Optional)

### Writing Exercise
Ask students to write:
1. The conversation Worf should have with Troi (300 words)
2. A career development plan for someone on their team (200 words)
3. What Worf should say to leadership to advocate for Troi (150 words)

### Small Group Work
Divide into groups:
- Group A: You are Troi. What do you really want and why?
- Group B: You are Worf's manager. What advice do you give him?
- Group C: You are the other engineers on the team. What do you think?

### Role-Play Exercise
Set up conversations:
- Person 1: Worf, Person 2: Troi (the initial conversation)
- Person 1: Worf, Person 2: His manager (asking for help)
- Person 1: Worf, Person 2: Peer manager (exploring openings)
