# Context
Six months ago, Acme Corp acquired Collective, a smaller startup that had built innovative distributed ledger technology that could enhance Carboni's capabilities. The acquisition was strategic: Collective's technology would accelerate Acme's roadmap by at least a year, and Collective's team of twelve engineers brought deep expertise in consensus algorithms and Byzantine fault tolerance.

Seven, who led the distributed systems team at Collective as tech lead, was promoted to engineering manager of the newly combined Distributed Ledger team at Acme. The team now consists of the twelve engineers from Collective and eight engineers from Acme's existing blockchain team. Seven was chosen for the role because of her technical expertise and her deep knowledge of the Collective codebase, which would become the foundation for the combined team's work.

The integration has been challenging. The Collective engineers are used to a highly autonomous, fast-moving culture where small teams made decisions quickly with minimal process. Code reviews were lightweight, deployment happened multiple times per day, and engineers had broad access to production systems. The team took pride in their efficiency and bias for action.

The Acme engineers come from a more structured environment with comprehensive code review processes, staged deployment pipelines, extensive testing requirements, and careful access controls. These practices evolved from hard-learned lessons about operating critical financial infrastructure for central banks. The Acme engineers value reliability and correctness above speed.

Tensions have emerged. Boimler, an engineer from Acme, blocked a pull request from Mariner, a former Collective engineer, because it lacked sufficient test coverage. Mariner was frustrated: "This is exactly the kind of bureaucracy that kills innovation. We shipped features every day at Collective without all this ceremony." Boimler responded that he'd seen production incidents caused by insufficient testing and wasn't going to compromise on quality standards.

In last week's team meeting, Seven proposed adopting Collective's lightweight code review process for experimental features while maintaining Acme's rigorous process for production code. This satisfied no one. The Collective engineers felt their culture was being eroded, while the Acme engineers worried about creating a two-tier system that would lead to confusion and incidents.

Janeway, the VP of Engineering, has noticed the strain. She's observed that the team members still largely sit with their original colleagues, that Slack conversations have split along old company lines, and that the combined team is moving slower than either team did independently. She's asked Seven to develop a plan to build a unified team culture that leverages the strengths of both groups.

# Challenge
Seven finds herself caught between two cultures, each with legitimate values and hard-won lessons. The Collective engineers feel like they're being assimilated into Acme's way of doing things and losing what made them successful. The Acme engineers feel like their practices, built from real operational experience, are being dismissed as bureaucracy. Seven needs to build a unified team culture, but she's not sure how to honor both perspectives while creating something new that works for everyone.

# Discussion Prompts
- What are the legitimate concerns and values on each side of this cultural divide? What can Seven learn from each perspective?
- How might Seven's background as a Collective engineer and her promotion to manager be influencing the team dynamics and her approach to this challenge?
- What does "unified team culture" actually mean? Is the goal to blend the two cultures, pick one, or create something new?
- Beyond code review processes, what other cultural differences might exist between the teams? How might those be creating friction?
- What concrete steps could Seven take to build relationships and trust across the former company lines?
- How should decisions about team practices and processes be made? Who should have input? How can Seven avoid this feeling like one side "winning" over the other?
- What role should Janeway play in supporting this integration? What support does Seven need from leadership?
- How can Seven leverage the diversity of perspectives as a strength rather than seeing it only as a source of conflict?
- What metrics or signals would indicate that the integration is succeeding? What would failure look like?
