# Context
Acme Corp's Verification Systems team, led by their manager Data, is responsible for the cryptographic verification protocols that ensure the integrity of carbon credit transactions on CarboniChain. The work requires deep expertise in cryptography, distributed systems, and consensus algorithms. Data was promoted to engineering manager six months ago after serving as the technical lead and principal architect of the verification system. His technical contributions were exceptional, and he was the obvious choice to lead the team.

As technical lead, Data was known for his precise, thorough code reviews and his ability to identify edge cases that others missed. He would often take on the most complex technical challenges himself, producing elegant solutions that became examples for the rest of the team. His documentation was impeccable, and he mentored junior engineers through detailed technical explanations.

Now as manager, Data has continued many of these same practices. He still writes code regularly, reviews every pull request in detail, and jumps in to solve the hardest technical problems. He prepares meticulously for one-on-ones, bringing detailed notes on each person's recent work and technical areas for improvement. He's created comprehensive documentation on team processes and technical standards.

However, several concerns have emerged. La Forge, a senior engineer on the team, has grown frustrated that Data often solves problems himself rather than creating space for others to struggle and learn. "I start investigating an issue, and by the time I dig into it, Data has already identified the root cause and proposed a solution," La Forge says. "I appreciate the help, but I'm not learning as much as I could."

Meanwhile, Yar, a mid-level engineer, has been struggling with work-life balance and hinted in her last one-on-one that she's feeling burned out. Data provided technical suggestions for managing her workload more efficiently but didn't explore the underlying issues. Two weeks later, Yar submitted her resignation, citing burnout. In her exit interview with HR, she mentioned that she "never felt like Data understood what I was going through."

Guinan, the Director of Engineering, recently gave Data feedback that he's "managing like a senior engineer, not like a manager." She noted that while his technical contributions are still strong, the team's growth has slowed. Engineers aren't being stretched with challenging problems, and Data isn't developing the broader people management skills he'll need as the team grows.

# Challenge
Data is confused by this feedback. He's working harder than ever, helping the team with technical challenges, and maintaining high standards. Isn't that what good management looks like? He wonders whether he's truly suited for management, or whether he should consider moving back to a technical role. How can he evolve his approach to focus more on growing others rather than solving problems himself?

# Discussion Prompts
- What are the key differences between being an effective technical lead and being an effective engineering manager? How do the skills and priorities differ?
- What is Data doing well? What should he continue doing? What should he stop doing or do differently?
- How might Data's strengths as an engineer be creating blind spots in his management approach?
- What people management skills does Data need to develop? What would "good" look like for each of these skills?
- How should Data have handled the situation with Yar differently? What might he have missed in those one-on-ones?
- What should Data do to create more growth opportunities for engineers like La Forge without completely stepping away from technical work?
- Is it realistic for Data to continue writing code and doing detailed technical work as a manager? How might his technical involvement need to change as the team grows?
- How can Guinan support Data's development as a manager? What specific guidance or resources might help?
